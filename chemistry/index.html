Chemistry for Mechanical Engineers and Chemical Lab Instrumentation

Engines, boilers, ships, bridges and gears all fail or succeed partly through chemistry. This lecture covers the reactions a mechanical engineer meets most often (burning fuel, metals corroding, carbon diffusing into steel) and then the instruments used to measure chemistry in the laboratory: how they work, how they are calibrated, and how much to trust their numbers.

1Where chemistry meets machines

A mechanical engineer does not need to be a chemist, but needs enough chemistry to design for it, specify materials and treatments, and read a laboratory report critically. Learning outcomes for this lecture:

  • Balance a combustion reaction, find the air–fuel ratio, flue gas composition and flame temperature.
  • Explain corrosion as an electrochemical cell and estimate a corrosion rate from current with Faraday's law.
  • Use Fick's second law to estimate case depth in carburising.
  • Build and use a calibration curve, and state detection limits and uncertainty.
  • Describe how a pH meter, bomb calorimeter and flue gas analyser work, and their main sources of error.

2Fuels and combustion

Stoichiometry and air–fuel ratio

Air is taken as 21% O2 and 79% N2 by volume, so each kmol of O2 brings 3.76 kmol of N2. For a fuel CxHyOz burned completely with a fraction e of excess air:

CxHyOz + (1 + e)ast(O2 + 3.76N2) ⟶ x CO2 + (y/2) H2O + e ast O2 + 3.76(1 + e)ast N2,   ast = x + y/4 − z/2
The air–fuel ratio by mass is AFR = 4.76(1 + e)ast × 28.97 / Mfuel. Petrol needs about 15 kg of air per kg of fuel. Real burners run with some excess air so that all the fuel meets oxygen; too much excess air just heats nitrogen and carries energy up the chimney. The equivalence ratio φ = 1/(1 + e) and lambda λ = 1 + e are the same idea in other words.

Heating value and flame temperature

The higher heating value (HHV) counts the heat released when the water in the products condenses; the lower heating value (LHV) assumes it leaves as vapour. Engines and most boilers cannot recover that latent heat, so LHV is the usual basis; condensing boilers get part of the difference back by cooling the flue gas below its dew point. The adiabatic flame temperature is the product temperature if all the heat goes into the products: it sets NOx formation and the thermal load on combustor walls.

LHV × Mfuel + Σ nairΔh(Tair) = Σ ni Δhi(Tad)    (per kmol of fuel; Δh from ideal-gas tables)
The calculator solves this for Tad with tabulated enthalpies, assuming complete combustion and no dissociation, so it overestimates real flame temperatures above about 2,000 K by 50 to 150 K.

3Corrosion

Corrosion is an electrochemical cell

Metal corrodes when it is the anode of a cell: atoms give up electrons and leave as ions (Fe ⟶ Fe2+ + 2e−). Those electrons are consumed at a cathode, usually by dissolved oxygen (O2 + 2H2O + 4e− ⟶ 4OH−) or, in acids, by hydrogen evolution. A cell needs four things: anode, cathode, an electrolyte (water, soil, condensate) and a metallic path. Remove any one and corrosion stops; every protection method attacks one of them.

Faraday's law: m = I t M/(n F),   corrosion rate CR [mm/year] = 3.27 × 10−3 × i [μA/cm²] × EW / ρ [g/cm³]
F = 96,485 C/mol, n = electrons per atom, EW = M/n the equivalent weight. For steel, 1 μA/cm² is about 0.012 mm per year. Because corrosion is a current, it can be measured electrically (linear polarisation resistance probes) and stopped electrically (cathodic protection).

Galvanic corrosion

When two different metals touch in an electrolyte, the one lower in the galvanic series becomes the anode and corrodes faster, while the other is protected. In seawater and other neutral waters the cathode reaction is usually limited by how fast oxygen can reach the surface, so the total cathodic current is proportional to the cathode area, and it all comes out of the anode. A small anode coupled to a large cathode (a steel rivet in a copper sheet) is the worst case; the reverse (copper rivets in a steel plate) is tolerable.

Galvanic couple in seawater

Protection

  • Design: avoid dissimilar metals in contact, or insulate them; keep anodes large; avoid crevices and water traps; allow drainage.
  • Barriers: paint, polymer coatings, galvanising (zinc also protects sacrificially where scratched).
  • Cathodic protection: make the whole structure a cathode, with sacrificial zinc, aluminium or magnesium anodes (ships, small pipelines) or an impressed current from a rectifier (long pipelines, jetties).
  • Environment: remove oxygen from boiler feedwater, control pH, add inhibitors.
  • Material choice: stainless steels and titanium form passive oxide films; they are excellent until the film breaks down in chloride crevices.

4Surface hardening by diffusion

Gears, camshafts and bearing races need a hard, wear-resistant surface over a tough core. Carburising heats low-carbon steel to about 900 to 950 °C in a carbon-rich atmosphere; carbon diffuses in, and quenching then turns the high-carbon case to hard martensite. Nitriding does the same with nitrogen at lower temperature. Diffusion into a thick part from a surface held at constant concentration follows Fick's second law:

(Cx − C0)/(Cs − C0) = 1 − erf(x / 2√(Dt)),   D = D0 exp(−Qd/RT)
For carbon in austenite, D0 = 2.3 × 10−5 m²/s and Qd = 148 kJ/mol (Callister & Rethwisch, 2018). Case depth grows only with √t, so doubling the depth takes four times as long; raising the temperature is far more effective because D rises exponentially.

5Measurement basics and calibration

What a chemical measurement needs

Most chemical instruments measure a voltage, a light intensity, a temperature rise or a current that depends on concentration. A calibration against standards of known composition turns that signal into a result.

TermMeaning
AccuracyCloseness to the true value. Its opposite is bias.
PrecisionCloseness of repeated readings to each other. A precise instrument can still be inaccurate.
SensitivitySlope of signal against concentration.
SelectivityHow little other substances interfere with the reading.
Limit of detection (LOD)Smallest concentration distinguishable from a blank, commonly 3.3 s/m (s: residual standard deviation, m: slope). The limit of quantification is 10 s/m.
TraceabilityStandards and instruments calibrated through an unbroken chain to national or international references.
UncertaintyA stated range within which the true value lies with a given confidence, combining every source of error.

Absorption spectrophotometry and the calibration curve

A UV–visible spectrophotometer passes light of one wavelength through a sample and measures the transmitted fraction T = I/I0. The absorbance follows the Beer–Lambert law, which is linear in concentration over a limited range:

A = −log10T = ε b c
ε is the molar absorptivity, b the path length (usually 1 cm) and c the concentration. Stray light reaching the detector makes the curve bend over at high absorbance, so samples above about A = 1 should be diluted. The same law underlies infrared gas analysers (section 7).

6The pH meter

pH = −log10aH+ controls corrosion, scaling and the life of coolants and lubricants. A pH meter is a high-impedance voltmeter connected to a glass electrode, whose thin glass membrane develops a potential that depends on the hydrogen ion activity outside it, and a reference electrode (usually silver/silver chloride) that holds a constant potential. The cell voltage follows the Nernst equation:

E = E0 − k (2.303RT/F)(pH − 7),   2.303RT/F = 59.16 mV per pH at 25 °C

The ideal slope changes with temperature (54.2 mV/pH at 0 °C, 64.1 at 50 °C), so meters use a temperature probe for automatic temperature compensation (ATC). Real electrodes have an offset (asymmetry potential) and a slope efficiency k below 100% that worsen with age; a two-point calibration with buffers measures both. Meters typically ask for the electrode to be cleaned or replaced when the slope falls below about 95% or the offset exceeds about ±30 mV. ATC corrects only the electrode slope, not the real change of the sample's own pH with temperature.

7Calorimetry and gas analysis

The bomb calorimeter

The heating value of a solid or liquid fuel is measured in an oxygen bomb calorimeter (ASTM D240 for liquid fuels, ISO 1928 for solid fuels). A weighed sample burns in pure oxygen at about 30 bar inside a steel vessel immersed in a known mass of water; the water temperature rise gives the heat released. The calorimeter's energy equivalent C (J/K) is found first by burning certified benzoic acid, whose heat of combustion is 26.454 MJ/kg. Because the products cool back to near room temperature and the water condenses, the bomb measures the gross (higher) heating value at constant volume; corrections are made for the ignition wire and for nitric and sulfuric acid formed.

C = (mBA × 26,454 + ewire)/ΔTcal,   HHV = (C ΔT − ewire − eacid)/m,   LHV ≃ HHV − 2.442 × 9 wH MJ/kg
wH is the mass fraction of hydrogen in the fuel: each kg of hydrogen makes 9 kg of water, and 2.442 MJ/kg is the latent heat of water at 25 °C.

Flue gas analysers

Boiler and engine tuning rely on measuring the exhaust. From the measured O2, a flue gas analyser works out excess air and CO2, and estimates the flue gas loss with the Siegert formula:

λ ≃ 21/(21 − O2),   CO2 = CO2,max(1 − O2/21),   qA = (Tflue − Tair)(A2/(21 − O2) + B) %
O2 and CO2 are on a dry basis because the water was removed. A2 and B are fuel constants. The formula gives the sensible heat loss on a net-calorific-value basis; it ignores the latent heat in the water vapour, which is why it can say a condensing boiler is over 100% efficient on an LHV basis.

Portable analysers draw a sample through a heated probe, cool and dry it, and pass it over sensors: electrochemical cells for O2, CO and NO, a thermocouple for flue temperature, and often an infrared cell for CO2. Electrochemical cells drift and are poisoned over time, so they are checked against certified test gases, typically every year. Laboratory and emissions-monitoring systems use non-dispersive infrared (NDIR) for CO and CO2, paramagnetic or zirconia sensors for O2, chemiluminescence for NOx and flame ionisation for hydrocarbons.

Good practice in the lab

  • Calibrate before use with standards that bracket the samples, and check with an independent control standard.
  • Record temperature: almost every chemical measurement depends on it.
  • Run blanks and replicates; report the mean with its uncertainty and the number of readings.
  • Sample correctly: a perfect analysis of an unrepresentative sample is worthless (take oil from a running system, not the bottom of the sump).
  • Read the safety data sheet for every reagent; wear eye protection; handle oxygen bombs, acids and flammable solvents as the procedure requires.
