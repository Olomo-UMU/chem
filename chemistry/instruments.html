1Viscometers

Principle

Viscosity is a fluid's resistance to shear, and it is the single most important property of a lubricant: it sets the thickness of the oil film in bearings and gears. Two quantities are used. Dynamic viscosity η (mPa⋅s, or centipoise) is shear stress divided by shear rate. Kinematic viscosity ν = η/ρ (mm²/s, or centistokes) is what gravity-driven instruments measure and what oil grades are based on.

Glass capillary: ν = C t,   η = ν ρ    Viscosity–temperature (ASTM D341): log log(ν + 0.7) = A − B log T
C is the tube's calibration constant (mm²/s²) and t the flow time between two marks. T is absolute temperature. Two measured points fix A and B, so viscosity at any operating temperature can be read off a straight line on the special D341 chart. The viscosity index (VI, ASTM D2270) expresses how flat that line is: higher VI means less thinning when hot.

Procedure: kinematic viscosity by ASTM D445

  1. Choose a calibrated capillary whose flow time will be at least 200 s.
  2. Filter the sample if it contains particles; charge the viscometer to the fill marks.
  3. Mount it vertically in the bath at 40.00 °C or 100.00 °C and wait about 30 minutes for it to reach temperature.
  4. Draw the liquid above the upper mark, release it, and time the meniscus between the two marks.
  5. Repeat; the two flow times must agree within the method's determinability (about 0.2 to 0.4% for clean oils).
  6. Calculate ν = C t from the mean time and report it to four significant figures with the temperature.

  • Research: new lubricants and bio-based oils, nanofluids for heat transfer, rheology of 3D-printing pastes and concrete, elastohydrodynamic film modelling (which needs pressure–viscosity data).
  • Errors: temperature (viscosity of oil changes 5 to 7% per °C near 40 °C), air bubbles, particles blocking the capillary, a tube not vertical, timing by eye.

2Conductivity meter

Principle

Dissolved salts, acids and bases carry current as ions, so electrical conductivity is a quick, cheap measure of how much dissolved material water contains. The meter applies an alternating voltage (to avoid polarising the electrodes) across a cell of known geometry and measures the conductance G.

κ = G Kcell,   κ25 = κT/[1 + α(T − 25)],   TDS ≃ k κ25
Kcell (cm⁻¹) is the cell constant: 0.01 to 0.1 for pure water, 1 for ordinary waters, 10 for brines. α is about 2%/°C for most natural waters, so readings are always reported at 25 °C. The factor k converting μS/cm to mg/L of total dissolved solids is about 0.55 to 0.75 depending on the salts present (0.64 is common).

Procedure

  1. Choose a cell constant suited to the expected range.
  2. Calibrate with a certified KCl standard, for example 0.01 mol/L KCl = 1,413 μS/cm at 25 °C; for pure water use a low-range standard or a factory-calibrated flow cell.
  3. Rinse the cell with the sample, immerse it with no trapped air, and let the temperature stabilise.
  4. Read the temperature-compensated value; for high-purity water measure in a closed flow cell, because carbon dioxide from the air raises the reading within seconds.
  5. For steam plant, measure cation conductivity after a hydrogen-form cation exchange column, which removes ammonia and amines so that only harmful anions (chloride, sulfate) are seen.

  • Errors: wrong temperature coefficient, polarised or fouled electrodes, air bubbles, CO2 absorption in pure water, a cell constant that is out of range.

3Titration

Principle

A titration adds a reagent of exactly known concentration (the titrant) to a sample until the reaction is just complete, the end point. The volume used gives the amount of the substance. The end point is found by a colour indicator or, in automatic titrators, by an electrode: the potential changes fastest at the equivalence point, so the instrument finds the maximum of dE/dV.

Base number: TBN = (V − Vblank) N × 56.1 / m   mg KOH/g    Hardness: mg/L as CaCO3 = VEDTA M × 100,090 / Vsample
V in mL, N the acid normality, m the sample mass in g, 56.1 the molar mass of KOH. For hardness, M is EDTA molarity and 100,090 mg/mol converts calcium-equivalent moles to mg of CaCO3.

Procedure: potentiometric TBN

  1. Weigh the oil sample (typically 1 to 5 g, less for high-TBN marine oils) into the titration beaker.
  2. Dissolve it in the specified solvent mixture and immerse the cleaned glass and reference electrodes.
  3. Titrate with standardised acid while the titrator records potential against volume, adding smaller increments near the end point.
  4. The instrument locates the inflection; run a blank on the solvent alone and subtract it.
  5. Standardise the titrant regularly against a primary standard and check the electrode response with buffers.

  • Errors: unstandardised titrant, dirty or slow electrodes, overshooting the end point, CO2 absorbed by alkaline titrants, sample not fully dissolved.

4Karl Fischer titrator

Principle

Water is the commonest contaminant in oils and fuels: it causes rust, breaks down additives, promotes microbial growth in fuel and lowers the dielectric strength of transformer oil. The Karl Fischer reaction consumes water exactly in a 1 : 1 ratio with iodine:

I2 + SO2 + H2O + 3RN + CH3OH ⟶ 2RNH⁺I⁻ + RNH⁺CH3SO4⁻    coulometric: 10.71 C of charge per mg of water
In the volumetric method, iodine-containing reagent is added from a burette (range about 100 ppm to 100%). In the coulometric method, iodine is generated electrically at an anode, and Faraday's law gives the water directly from the charge: 2 × 96,485 C per 18.015 g of water. It reaches 1 ppm and needs no titrant standardisation, so it is the standard for oils (ASTM D6304). Oils with additives that react with the reagent are heated in an oven and the water is carried into the cell by dry gas.

Procedure: coulometric, ASTM D6304

  1. Fill the cell with fresh anolyte and catholyte and let the instrument titrate the cell dry; wait for a low, stable drift (typically below 10 to 20 μg/min).
  2. Shake the sample thoroughly (water settles), draw it into a dry syringe and weigh the syringe.
  3. Inject through the septum below the liquid surface and reweigh the syringe to get the sample mass by difference.
  4. The instrument generates iodine until the indicator electrode shows excess, and reports water corrected for drift.
  5. Check the system with a certified water standard; replace reagent when it is exhausted or the drift will not settle.

  • Research: water ingress in seals and bearings, ageing of transformer insulation (water in paper and oil), hygroscopic bio-lubricants, battery electrolytes (lithium cells need below about 20 ppm).
  • Errors: unrepresentative sample (water settles or clings to walls), moisture from air or wet syringes, side reactions with additives, exhausted reagent, high drift.

5AAS and ICP-OES spectrometers

Principle

Both measure metals by the light that free atoms absorb or emit at wavelengths unique to each element. In atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS), the sample is sprayed into a flame (or dried and atomised in a graphite furnace) and light from a lamp made of the element being measured passes through it; absorbance follows the Beer–Lambert law. In inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), the sample enters an argon plasma at about 6,000 to 10,000 K, every element emits its own lines, and a spectrometer measures dozens at once. ICP-MS separates ions by mass instead and reaches parts per trillion.

Procedure: wear metals in used oil by ICP-OES (ASTM D5185)

  1. Shake the sample well; dilute a weighed amount (typically 1 : 10) in kerosene or xylene with an internal standard such as yttrium or cobalt.
  2. Calibrate with oil-based multi-element standards (for example 0, 10, 50, 100 mg/kg) and a blank.
  3. Aspirate samples; the instrument measures each element's emission lines and corrects with the internal standard for viscosity and nebuliser effects.
  4. Run a check standard every 10 to 20 samples; recalibrate if it drifts beyond about ±5 to 10%.
  5. Report mg/kg (ppm) for each element and compare with previous samples from the same machine.

Spectrometric oil analysis sees only small particles (below about 5 to 10 μm). Large wear debris from a failing gear or bearing can be missed, so programmes add particle counting, ferrography or a magnetic plug.

Uses in industry and research

  • Industry: condition monitoring of engines, gearboxes, turbines and hydraulics in mining, aviation, rail, shipping and power; trace metals in drinking water and effluent; alloy and coating composition; catalyst and fuel analysis (vanadium, nickel, sodium in heavy fuel oil).
  • Research: wear mechanisms in tribology tests, leaching from materials, battery recycling (lithium, cobalt, nickel recovery), heavy metals in soils and in biochar, nanoparticle dissolution.
  • Errors: matrix and viscosity effects, spectral interferences between lines, particles too large to atomise, contamination from containers, poor sample shaking.

6FTIR spectrometer

Principle

Chemical bonds vibrate at characteristic frequencies and absorb infrared light at those frequencies. A Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer passes broadband infrared through the sample via a moving-mirror interferometer and converts the interferogram into a spectrum by Fourier transform. Peaks are reported in wavenumbers (cm⁻¹, from about 4,000 to 400). With an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) crystal, solids and liquids are simply pressed onto the crystal with no preparation.

Procedure: identifying a polymer by ATR-FTIR

  1. Clean the ATR crystal with solvent and record a background spectrum of the clean crystal.
  2. Press the sample firmly onto the crystal with the clamp; for a moulded part, cut a fresh surface.
  3. Collect the spectrum (typically 16 to 32 scans at 4 cm⁻¹ resolution).
  4. Identify the main peaks and compare with a reference library; the software gives a match quality.
  5. For in-service oil (ASTM E2412), use a transmission cell of fixed path length and compare against the new oil's spectrum.

  • Errors: poor contact with the ATR crystal, an old background, water vapour and CO2 peaks from the room, saturated (too strong) bands in transmission cells.

7Gas chromatograph

Principle

A gas chromatograph separates a mixture by carrying it in an inert gas (helium, hydrogen or nitrogen) through a long, thin column whose coating holds each component back by a different amount. Components leave at different retention times, which identify them, and the area of each peak gives the amount. Common detectors are the flame ionisation detector (FID), very sensitive to hydrocarbons, and the thermal conductivity detector (TCD), which responds to every gas including H2, O2, N2, CO and CO2. Coupled to a mass spectrometer (GC-MS) it identifies unknowns.

Resolution Rs = 2(tR2 − tR1)/(w1 + w2),   amounti = RFi × Areai
Rs ≥ 1.5 means baseline separation. Response factors RF come from calibration gases of certified composition.

Procedure: natural gas analysis (ISO 6974) and dissolved gas analysis (IEC 60567)

  1. Condition the column and check the baseline; inject a certified calibration gas and confirm retention times and response factors.
  2. For gas: purge the sample loop with sample gas and inject a fixed volume through a gas sampling valve.
  3. For transformer oil: sample oil into a gas-tight syringe without air, extract the dissolved gases (headspace or vacuum), then inject them.
  4. Integrate the peaks; calculate each component's concentration; run a duplicate.
  5. For natural gas, compute calorific value, Wobbe index and density from the composition (ISO 6976).

Dissolved gas analysis: diagnosing transformer faults

Electrical and thermal faults inside an oil-filled transformer break down the oil and produce characteristic gases. Their relative proportions point to the fault type. The Duval triangle (IEC 60599) uses the relative percentages of methane, ethylene and acetylene.

8Thermogravimetric analysis and DSC

Principle

A thermogravimetric analyser (TGA) weighs a few milligrams of sample on a microbalance while a furnace heats it on a programme, in nitrogen or air. Steps in the mass curve show moisture leaving, volatile matter driven off, and finally combustion of carbon, leaving ash. A differential scanning calorimeter (DSC) measures the heat flow into a sample compared with an empty reference pan, revealing melting, crystallisation, glass transition and curing reactions.

Fixed carbon = 100 − moisture − volatile matter − ash    Crystallinity Xc = ΔHm/ΔHm0
ΔHm0, the melting enthalpy of a 100% crystalline polymer, is about 293 J/g for polyethylene, 207 J/g for polypropylene and 140 J/g for PET.

Procedure: proximate analysis of a fuel by TGA (after ASTM D7582)

  1. Grind the sample to below about 250 μm and weigh 10 to 20 mg (micro-TGA) or about 1 g (macro-TGA) into a crucible.
  2. Heat in nitrogen to about 107 °C and hold until the mass is constant: the loss is moisture.
  3. Heat in nitrogen to 900 to 950 °C and hold: the further loss is volatile matter.
  4. Cool to about 600 to 750 °C and switch to air or oxygen: the carbon burns; the remaining mass is ash.
  5. Fixed carbon is the difference. Run duplicates; calibrate the balance and temperature with reference materials.

  • Errors: sample not representative (heterogeneous fuels), buoyancy drift, too fast a heating rate overlapping steps, oxygen leaking into the nitrogen purge.

9Potentiostat

Principle

A potentiostat controls the potential of a metal sample (the working electrode) relative to a reference electrode (saturated calomel or silver/silver chloride) and measures the current that flows to a counter electrode (platinum or graphite). Because corrosion is a current, sweeping the potential a little around the free corrosion potential Ecorr reveals the corrosion rate within minutes instead of the months a weight-loss test needs.

i = icorr[e2.303(E − Ecorr)/βa − e−2.303(E − Ecorr)/βc],   icorr = B/Rp,   B = βaβc/[2.303(βa + βc)]
βa and βc are the Tafel slopes (mV per decade). Linear polarisation resistance (LPR, ASTM G59) measures Rp = ΔE/Δi over ±10 to 20 mV, which does not damage the sample. Tafel extrapolation sweeps ±250 mV and extends the straight parts of log|i| back to Ecorr. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) applies small AC signals over a range of frequencies to study coatings and films. ASTM G102 converts icorr to a corrosion rate.

Procedure: polarisation test

  1. Polish the specimen to a standard finish, measure its exposed area, degrease and mount it.
  2. Fill the cell with the test solution (for example 3.5% NaCl), insert the three electrodes, and record the open-circuit potential until stable (often 30 to 60 minutes).
  3. Run LPR: sweep from −15 to +15 mV about Ecorr at 0.1 to 0.2 mV/s and take Rp from the slope at zero current.
  4. If needed, run a potentiodynamic sweep from −250 mV to +250 mV (or further, to find pitting) on a fresh specimen.
  5. Fit Tafel slopes, calculate icorr and convert it to mm/year; repeat on at least three specimens.

  • Research: new alloys and coatings, inhibitors from plant extracts, microbially influenced corrosion, hydrogen embrittlement, batteries, supercapacitors, electrolysers and fuel cells (the same instrument runs cyclic voltammetry and charge–discharge tests).
  • Errors: uncompensated solution resistance (IR drop) in low-conductivity water, wrong exposed area, crevices at the specimen mount, sweeping too fast, assuming Tafel behaviour where there is none.

10Salt spray cabinet

Principle

A salt spray (salt fog) cabinet exposes coated or plated parts to a continuous fine mist of salt solution at a controlled temperature, to compare the corrosion resistance of coatings in a few days or weeks. The neutral salt spray test, ASTM B117 and ISO 9227 (NSS), is the most widely specified accelerated corrosion test in the world.

ASTM B117 / ISO 9227 NSS conditionValue
Salt solution5 ± 1% sodium chloride in pure water
Solution pH (collected fog)6.5 to 7.2
Chamber temperature35 ± 2 °C
Fog collection rate1.0 to 2.0 mL per hour per 80 cm² of horizontal area
Specimen angle15 to 30° from vertical

Procedure

  1. Clean the specimens as specified; for painted panels, cut a scribe through the coating to bare metal.
  2. Mask cut edges and contact points; place panels in racks at the specified angle so drips do not fall from one onto another.
  3. Check the collection rate and pH daily; record any interruptions.
  4. Expose for the specified hours (24, 96, 240, 500, 1,000 or more).
  5. Remove, rinse gently, dry, and rate: time to first white or red rust, blistering (ASTM D714), creep from the scribe in mm (ASTM D1654), rust grade (ISO 4628).

Typical results and limits

Results are specified by each customer standard for its own coating system; a few examples of the kinds of requirement seen:

  • Zinc electroplating with passivation: often rated in tens to a few hundred hours to white rust, with requirements rising with zinc thickness and passivation type.
  • Zinc–nickel plating for automotive fasteners: commonly specified in the range of several hundred to about 1,000 hours to red rust.
  • Powder-coated or painted steel: often 500 to 1,000 hours with scribe creep limited to a few millimetres.

Hours in a salt spray cabinet cannot be converted into years of service. Continuous wet salt fog does not reproduce wet–dry cycling, sunlight, or pollutants, and it ranks some coatings wrongly (it is particularly unrepresentative for zinc, whose protective layer needs drying cycles). Cyclic tests such as ASTM G85, SAE J2334 and ISO 12944-9 correlate better with outdoor exposure and are increasingly specified.

Uses in industry and research

  • Industry: quality control of plating and paint lines, supplier approval, automotive, aerospace, marine and electrical enclosure specifications.
  • Research: ranking new coatings and inhibitors, comparing surface treatments, combined with EIS and outdoor exposure sites to build service-life models.
  • Errors: incorrect fog rate or pH, contaminated salt, panels touching or dripping on each other, inconsistent scribing, subjective rating.
