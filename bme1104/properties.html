1.1Chemistry and the mechanical engineer

Chemistry studies matter at the scale of atoms and molecules. What happens at that scale explains what we see at the scale of a machine: why steel is strong, why copper conducts, why a plastic softens on a hot day. An engineer uses these large-scale properties, such as hardness, strength, malleability and conductivity, to choose the right material for a job, and often measures them in the laboratory.

Chemistry matters to a mechanical engineer in at least ten ways:

  • Material selection and design. The atomic and molecular structure decides how a material behaves at high temperature, in corrosive surroundings or in contact with chemicals.
  • Manufacturing processes. Heat treatment, surface coating and material synthesis are chemical processes.
  • Material synthesis. Composites and polymers are made by chemical reactions.
  • Corrosion prevention. Knowing the reactions behind corrosion lets you choose materials, coatings and inhibitors.
  • Environmental interaction. Water, air and chemicals act on every material in service.
  • Fuel and combustion. Engines, boilers and power plants run on the chemistry of fuels.
  • Chemical thermodynamics. Heat and reaction energy matter in refrigeration, heating and power generation.
  • Developing new materials with high strength, low weight or resistance to extreme conditions.
  • Advanced manufacturing. Nanotechnology and additive manufacturing (3D printing) rely on chemical processes.
  • Hazardous materials and sustainability. Safe handling, storage and disposal, and choosing materials with a smaller environmental impact.

1.2Chemical properties of materials

Chemical properties describe how a material reacts with other substances at the atomic or molecular level. They decide how a part behaves in a corrosive or reactive environment. The main ones are reactivity, corrosion resistance, oxidation, pH reactivity and toxicity.

Reactivity

Reactivity is the tendency of a material to take part in a chemical reaction with oxygen, water, acids or bases. Oxidation reactions turn metals into oxides (rust and tarnish). Hydrolysis is reaction with water, which degrades many polymers and some ceramics. Reaction with acids and bases corrodes or etches metals: aluminium, for example, reacts with acids. Stainless steel is chosen for corrosive service because its reactivity is low.

Corrosion resistance

Corrosion resistance is the ability to withstand damage by oxidation or other reactions with the surroundings. Electrochemical corrosion works like a small battery on the metal surface and causes pitting, galvanic and crevice corrosion. Chemical corrosion comes from high temperature or direct attack by chemicals. It matters most in marine structures, chemical plants and buried pipelines, where titanium, stainless steel and some polymers are valued.

Oxidation

Oxidation is reaction with oxygen to form an oxide. Iron forms iron oxide (rust), which flakes off and exposes fresh metal. Aluminium forms a thin, tight film of aluminium oxide that protects the metal under it. Polymers also oxidise over time and lose strength. Whether an oxide film protects can be judged from the Pilling–Bedworth ratio, the volume of oxide formed divided by the volume of metal used up:

PBR=Vmetal​Voxide​​=nMmetal​ρoxide​Moxide​ρmetal​​
M = molar mass, ρ = density, n = number of metal atoms in one formula unit of the oxide (2 for Al₂O₃).

Will the oxide protect the metal?

A ratio below about 1 gives a porous film that does not cover the metal (magnesium). A ratio between 1 and 2 gives a continuous film that protects (aluminium, chromium, nickel, titanium). A ratio well above 2 gives a film that builds up stress, cracks and flakes off (iron rust, tungsten). This is one reason stainless steel contains chromium.

Acidity or basicity (pH) reactivity

Some materials react strongly at low or high pH. Zinc and magnesium corrode quickly in acids. Aluminium forms a protective layer in some environments but dissolves in strong alkalis. Some polymers and ceramics lose their strength at extreme pH. This governs the choice of materials in chemical processing, waste treatment and containers for acids and bases.

Toxicity

Toxicity is the degree to which a material, or what it breaks down into, harms people or the environment. Lead, mercury and asbestos were once widely used and are now restricted. PVC gives off harmful gases when it burns. Toxicity matters most in medical devices, food packaging and consumer products, and in rules for disposal.

1.3Physical properties of materials

Physical properties can be observed or measured without changing the chemical composition of the material. They include density, melting and boiling points, thermal conductivity, electrical conductivity, optical properties and magnetic properties.

  • Density is mass per unit volume, in kg/m³. It depends on the atoms present and how tightly they pack, and for gases on temperature and pressure. Low density matters in aircraft and vehicles; high density in counterweights.
  • Melting point (solid to liquid) matters in casting, welding and high-temperature service. Boiling point (liquid to gas) matters in heat exchangers and refrigeration. Tungsten melts at about 3422 °C; polyethylene at about 115 to 135 °C.
  • Thermal conductivity k, in W/(m K), is the ability to conduct heat. Copper and aluminium conduct well and are used in heat exchangers; glass fibre, foams and ceramics insulate.
  • Electrical conductivity σ, in S/m, is the ability to carry an electric current. Copper and silver carry current in wiring and circuit boards; rubber, glass and ceramics insulate.
  • Optical properties: transparency (glass, polymers), reflectivity (aluminium, silver) and the refractive index n = c/v, the speed of light in a vacuum divided by its speed in the material, which sets how light bends in lenses and optical fibres.
  • Magnetic properties: ferromagnetic materials (iron, nickel, cobalt) are strongly attracted and can be magnetised, for motors and transformers; paramagnetic (aluminium) and diamagnetic (copper) materials are only weakly attracted or repelled.

1.4Mechanical properties of materials

Mechanical properties describe how a material behaves under force. They decide whether a part survives the loads it meets in service. The tensile test measures most of them at once: a specimen is pulled until it breaks while the load and extension are recorded. The engineering stress and strain are

σ=A0​F​,ε=L0​ΔL​,E=εσ​  (elastic region)
F = load, A₀ = original cross-section area, ΔL = extension of a gauge length L₀, E = Young's modulus.

Tensile test

  • Strength is the ability to carry a force without failing or deforming permanently. Tensile strength resists pulling apart (steel in bridges), compressive strength resists crushing (concrete in columns), shear strength resists sliding (bolts and rivets). It is given in pascals (Pa) or megapascals (MPa).
  • Elasticity is the ability to return to the original shape when the load is removed, up to the elastic limit or yield point. Rubber is highly elastic; steel has a clear elastic limit, which suits springs.
  • Plasticity is permanent deformation without breaking, used in forging, rolling and extrusion.
  • Ductility is how far a material deforms plastically before it breaks, measured as percentage elongation or reduction of area. Copper and aluminium are ductile; cast iron is not.
  • Toughness is the energy a material absorbs before it breaks, a mix of strength and ductility. It is the area under the stress–strain curve, and is measured in impact tests (Charpy and Izod).
  • Hardness is resistance to permanent indentation or scratching, and so to wear. It is measured by the Brinell, Rockwell and Vickers tests. Cutting tools, gears and bearings need it.
  • Fatigue resistance is resistance to failure under repeated loading. Fatigue cracks grow at stresses below the tensile strength, starting at notches, holes, sharp corners and rough surfaces.
  • Creep resistance is resistance to slow deformation under a steady load, mainly at high temperature, in turbines, engines, boilers and reactors.

Impact test for toughness

In a Charpy test a heavy pendulum is released from a height, strikes a notched bar, and swings on. The energy used to break the bar is the potential energy lost:

Eabs​=mgR(cosβ−cosα)
m = pendulum mass, R = distance from the pivot to the centre of the striker, α = release angle, β = angle it rises to after the break (both measured from the bottom).

Charpy impact tester

Drag to turn, scroll or pinch to zoom. Try mild steel at −40 °C and at +20 °C.

Many steels are tough at room temperature but brittle in the cold: they have a ductile-to-brittle transition. Ships and pipelines have failed because of it, which is why impact tests are run at the lowest service temperature. Aluminium and copper alloys (face-centred cubic metals) do not show the transition.

Hardness, fatigue and creep

The Brinell test presses a hardened ball of diameter D into the surface with a load P and measures the diameter d of the dent:
