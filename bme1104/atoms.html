2.1The structure of the atom

Atoms are made of three kinds of particle: electrons, protons and neutrons. Positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons are held tightly together in the small nucleus at the centre. Negatively charged electrons occupy the space around it, forming the electron cloud. Protons and neutrons carry almost all of the mass, so the nucleus holds almost all of the mass of the atom, while the electrons take up almost all of its volume.

2.2Elementary particles and terms

The electron

The electron carries a negative charge of 1.602 × 10−19 C, equal in size to the charge on a proton. It behaves both as a tiny particle and as a wave with the de Broglie wavelength

λ=mvh​
h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s (Planck's constant), m = mass, v = speed. For electrons in experiments, λ is of the order of 0.1 nm, the size of an atom.

By Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, the momentum and position of an electron cannot both be known exactly. The product of the uncertainties cannot be smaller than a fixed value (the notes use h/2π; the strict modern form uses h/4π):

Δp⋅Δx ≥ 4πh​

So an orbital cannot be pictured as a sharp path around the nucleus: it is a cloud of probability where the electron is likely to be found. In Bohr's model the electron is limited to fixed orbits (quantum states), each with its own energy. The electrons in the outermost shell decide most engineering properties: bonding, electrical and optical behaviour and chemical reactivity.

The proton and the neutron

The proton is the nucleus of the hydrogen atom. It carries a unit positive charge of 1.602 × 10−19 C and has a mass of 1.673 × 10−27 kg. The neutron is electrically neutral and slightly heavier, 1.675 × 10−27 kg. Protons and neutrons together are called nucleons. A free neutron decays in about 15 minutes into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino, but it is not made of them. The number of protons Z is the charge number or atomic number, the number of neutrons is N, and their sum is the mass number:

A=Z+N
For light nuclei N is about equal to Z; for heavy nuclei N/Z rises to about 1.5.

Other particles include the positron (a positive electron), the neutrino and antineutrino, the mesons, the deuteron and the alpha particle.

Atomic number, atomic weight and mass number

The atomic number Z is the number of protons in the nucleus, the positive charge on it. All atoms of one element have the same Z, which sets its place in the periodic table. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons: iron has Z = 26, so 26 protons and 26 electrons.

The atomic weight (relative atomic mass) is the average mass of the atoms of an element compared with one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Older texts used one sixteenth of an oxygen atom. It is not the same as the mass number A, the count of protons and neutrons in one nucleus. Most atomic weights are not whole numbers because elements are mixtures of isotopes. Atomic masses are given in atomic mass units:

1 u=1.6605×10−24 g=1.6605×10−27 kg

Isotopes, isobars and isotones

  • Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons, so different masses. Hydrogen has three: ordinary hydrogen X11​X2121​H, deuterium X12​X2122​D and tritium X13​X2123​T. Chlorine has X1735​X217235​Cl and X1737​X217237​Cl in the ratio of about 3 to 1.
  • Isobars have the same mass number but belong to different elements: argon-40 (18 p, 22 n) and calcium-40 (20 p, 20 n).
  • Isotones have the same number of neutrons but different Z and A: X613​X26213​C and X714​X27214​N both have 7 neutrons.

The atomic weight is the average of the isotope masses weighted by their abundance:

For chlorine: (3 × 35 + 1 × 37)/4 = 35.5, close to the tabulated 35.45.

Avogadro's number and the nucleus

Avogadro's number NA​=6.022×1023 mol−1 is the number of atoms in one mole (in a mass in grams equal to the atomic weight). It links the atomic scale to the scale of the laboratory.

Rutherford's scattering of alpha particles by gold foil (about 1911) showed that nearly all the mass of an atom is in a tiny positive nucleus, less than 1/10 000 of the size of the atom. Nuclear radii are about R=1.2×10−15A1/3 m, so nuclear matter has a density of about 2×1017 kg/m3, some 1014 times that of ordinary matter. Matter in bulk is mostly empty space.

2.3Bohr's model, shells and orbitals

In Bohr's model electrons move in circular orbits of fixed energy at fixed distances from the nucleus, and cannot exist between them. The energy is quantised. Each orbit has a principal quantum number n = 1, 2, 3 and so on, counting outwards; the energy rises with n. For a one-electron atom the energies are

En​=−n213.6 eV Z2​
Z = nuclear charge. When an electron drops from a higher level to a lower one it gives out a photon of energy hf = E_upper − E_lower.

Energy levels and the light an atom gives out

X-ray spectra showed that each orbit holds more than one electron, at slightly different energies. Each principal level became an electron shell, also lettered K, L, M, N (n = 1, 2, 3, 4), and each shell was split into subshells s, p, d and f (from the sharp, principal, diffuse and fundamental lines of alkali-metal spectra), in order of increasing energy. A shell contains as many subshells as its value of n. Each subshell has an azimuthal quantum number l = 0, 1, 2, 3 and contains 2l + 1 orbitals.

Each orbital holds at most two electrons, and they must have opposite spin, with spin quantum number s = +½ or −½. The full description of an electron needs all four quantum numbers n, l, m and s, and by the Pauli exclusion principle no two electrons in an atom share all four. So the K shell (n = 1) has one s orbital and holds 2 electrons; the L shell (n = 2) has s and p subshells, four orbitals and 8 electrons; in general a shell holds 2n2.

2.4Electron assignments

Electrons fill the orbitals in order of increasing energy, each going into the lowest-energy orbital still free. This is the Aufbau principle, guided by two rules:

  1. The Pauli exclusion principle: no more than two electrons in any orbital, with opposite spins.
  2. Hund's rule: within a subshell, electrons go into the orbitals one at a time with parallel spins before any orbital takes a second electron.

The result is written in subshell notation: the shell number, the subshell letter and the number of electrons as a superscript, for example 1s22s22p63s1 for sodium. For elements 1 to 18 the order is simply 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p. From element 19 the 4s subshell fills before 3d, because the energy follows Madelung's rule: subshells fill in order of increasing n + l, and for equal n + l the one with the lower n fills first (3p and 4s both have n + l = 4; 3d has 5).

2.5The periodic table

The periodic table rests on Moseley's periodic law: the physical and chemical properties of the elements recur in a regular way when the elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number. It groups the elements by their electron configurations, which is what lets chemists predict how elements combine and react.

  • The vertical columns are the 18 groups. Elements in a group share chemical properties. Group 1 holds the alkali metals, group 2 the alkaline earth metals, group 17 the halogens, group 18 the noble gases, and groups 3 to 12 the transition metals.
  • The seven horizontal rows are the periods. The period number is the principal quantum number of the outermost occupied shell.
  • The table is divided into blocks named after the subshell being filled: the s-block (groups 1, 2 and helium), the p-block (groups 13 to 18), the d-block (groups 3 to 12) and the f-block (lanthanides and actinides, usually drawn below the table).
  • The number of valence electrons equals the group number for groups 1 and 2, and the group number minus 10 for groups 13 to 18.

2.6Periodic trends

Many properties rise or fall steadily across a period or down a group, and the changes in electron configuration explain them. The most useful are:

  • Atomic radius grows down a group (one more shell each period) and shrinks across a period (more protons pull the same shell in closer).
  • Ionisation energy, the energy to remove the outermost electron, falls down a group and rises across a period, peaking at the noble gases.
  • Electron affinity, the energy released when an atom gains an electron, generally becomes larger across a period towards the halogens.
  • Electronegativity, the pull of an atom on the electrons in a bond, rises across a period and falls down a group. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (3.98).
