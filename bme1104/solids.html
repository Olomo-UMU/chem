6.1Classification of solids

Solids keep a fixed shape and volume because they are extended arrays of atoms, ions or molecules held together by bonds. There are two main kinds. Crystalline solids have a long-range, highly ordered three-dimensional arrangement; amorphous solids lack long-range order.

6.2Crystalline solids and unit cells

A crystalline solid forms by crystallisation: precipitation from a concentrated solution, freezing of a liquid or deposition from a gas. Its ordered inside shows on the outside as flat faces meeting at definite angles. When X-rays pass through a crystal they are diffracted by the regular planes of particles; the angles and intensities of the diffracted beams (the diffraction pattern) reveal the positions of the atoms. This is X-ray crystallography.

The structure is built from a small repeating block, the unit cell, whose corners are lattice points. Repeated in three dimensions, the unit cell gives the crystal lattice. A unit cell is the simplest repeating unit; its opposite faces are parallel; and its edges connect equivalent points in neighbouring cells.

There are seven crystal systems, set by the edge lengths a, b, c and the angles α, β, γ between them. Each can occur in up to four forms: simple (particles at the corners only), body-centred (one more at the centre), face-centred (one more at the centre of each face) and base-centred (one more at the centre of two opposite faces). Together these make the 14 Bravais lattices.

Density from the unit cell

The density of a crystal equals the density of its unit cell: the mass of the particles in one cell divided by the volume of the cell. The mass of one particle is its molar mass divided by Avogadro's number, so for n particles per cell

ρ=NA​Vc​nM​
n = particles per cell, M = molar mass (g/mol), N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ /mol, V_c = cell volume (a³ for a cubic cell, in cm³; 1 pm = 10⁻¹⁰ cm).

The notes write this as M × NA × n / Vc; the molar mass must be divided by Avogadro's number to give the mass of one particle.

6.3Ionic solids

Ionic solids are not collections of ion pairs but huge lattices in which each ion is surrounded by several ions of opposite charge. The ions pack so as to maximise attractions and minimise repulsions. The number of nearest neighbours of opposite charge is the coordination number. In NaCl each NaX+ has six ClX− neighbours and, since the formula is 1:1, each ClX− has six NaX+: coordination 6:6. In AlX2​OX3​ each AlX3+ has six OX2− neighbours, so to keep the 2:3 ratio each OX2− has four AlX3+: coordination 6:4.

The most stable arrangements leave the least empty space: close-packed structures, most often face-centred cubic. The larger anions sit on the lattice points and the smaller cations fill the gaps. A cation in an octahedral hole has six anion neighbours; one in a tetrahedral hole has four. Which structure forms depends mainly on the ratio of the ionic radii.

The lattice energy is the energy released when the gaseous ions come together to form the crystal. It sums all the attractions and repulsions in the lattice and follows Coulomb's law:

E=knAr1​+r2​q1​q2​​
n = number of ion pairs, A = Madelung constant set by the structure (1.748 for NaCl, 1.763 for CsCl), r₁ + r₂ = bond length. It cannot be measured directly but is found from a Born–Haber cycle.

So ionic solids have high melting points, higher for larger charges and smaller ions: NaF (charges ±1) melts at 996 °C and MgO (±2) at 2852 °C; NaI, with the much larger iodide ion, melts at only 661 °C.

Compounds with charges of ±2 sit far to the right of those with ±1, and their melting points are far higher.

Properties of ionic solids

Ionic solids are hard, because the ions are strongly held, but brittle. If a force shifts one plane of ions by half a spacing, ions of like charge come face to face, repel, and the crystal splits along that plane, giving new flat faces at the same angles as the old. This is cleavage. In a metal the same shift leaves every ion surrounded by the same electron sea, so the metal bends instead of breaking. Solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity, because their ions cannot move; molten or dissolved, they conduct well.

6.4Molecular solids

Molecular solids are made of molecules (or, for the frozen noble gases, atoms) held by intermolecular forces. The bonds inside the molecules are strong, but the forces between them are weak, so molecular solids melt below about 300 °C, and most such substances are gases or liquids at room temperature. Melting points rise in the order nonpolar < polar < hydrogen-bonded.

  • Nonpolar molecular solids (noble gases, HX2​, NX2​, the halogens, methane, wax) are held by London forces, which fall off as the seventh power of the distance (F∝1/r7), so the molecules pack as closely as their shapes allow. The noble gases and solid methane form face-centred cubic crystals; many of these solids sublime. Large chain molecules such as pentane (CX5​HX12​) pack in more complicated cells.
  • Polar molecular solids arrange their molecules so that the δ+ end of one faces the δ− end of the next.
  • Hydrogen-bonded solids such as ice have the highest melting points of the three.

Molecular solids are generally soft and are electrical insulators, because their electrons are held in the covalent bonds. Their properties can depend on direction, because the forces between molecules are directional.

6.5Atomic (network) solids

In atomic or network solids the atoms are joined by covalent bonds into a continuous network. In three-dimensional networks (diamond, cubic boron nitride, quartz, silicon carbide) the whole crystal is one giant molecule, and any deformation means breaking strong bonds: these solids are extremely hard, melt at very high temperatures and are electrical insulators, though many conduct heat well. In two-dimensional networks (graphite, hexagonal boron nitride, mica) strong covalent sheets are stacked and held by weak London forces, so the sheets slide easily and the solid is soft. The common examples are the allotropes of carbon:

  • Diamond: each carbon is bonded to four others at the corners of a tetrahedron (sp3). The unit cell is face-centred cubic with four more atoms in half of the tetrahedral holes. Hardest natural material (Mohs 10).
  • Graphite: each carbon is bonded to three others in flat sheets of hexagonal rings (sp2), 142 pm apart within a sheet, with the sheets 335 pm apart. The fourth electron of each carbon is delocalised over the sheet, so graphite conducts electricity along the sheets but hardly at all across them. It is used as a dry lubricant and for electrodes and brushes in motors.
  • Other allotropes are the fullerenes (CX60​), carbon nanotubes and graphene, a single graphite sheet.

Use the unit-cell viewer in section 6.2 to compare the diamond and graphite structures. Cut along their crystal planes, three-dimensional network crystals such as diamond and zirconia (ZrOX2​) reflect light strongly and sparkle.

6.6Metallic solids

Metals crystallise mostly in the body-centred cubic, face-centred cubic and hexagonal close-packed structures; a few heavier elements of groups 13 to 15 form tetragonal (In, Sn), orthorhombic (Ga) or monoclinic (Bi) crystals. In metallic bonding, the valence orbitals of all the atoms overlap to form a vast number of molecular orbitals spread over the whole crystal, the conduction band. The valence electrons in it are delocalised: free to move through the solid. The crystal is held together by the attraction between the positive metal ions and this "sea" of electrons.

  • Electrical and thermal conductivity are high because the delocalised electrons carry charge and kinetic energy. Group 11 (Cu, Ag, Au), with a single s electron outside a full d subshell, conducts best (about 6×107 S/m and 400 W/(m K) for Cu and Ag).
  • Melting point and hardness follow the strength of the metallic bond, which is greatest when the valence subshells are about half full. Melting points rise from group 1 (caesium melts at 28 °C) to group 6 (tungsten at 3422 °C) and then fall.
  • Metals are malleable and ductile, because planes of atoms can slip past each other without breaking the bonding.

Alloys

Alloys are homogeneous mixtures of metals (and sometimes non-metals) of variable composition. In a substitutional alloy some host atoms are replaced by atoms of similar size: brass is copper with about a third of its atoms replaced by zinc; pewter is tin with some copper, bismuth and antimony. In an interstitial alloy small atoms sit in the holes between the host atoms: in steel, carbon atoms occupy holes in the iron lattice. Up to a point, more carbon gives a stronger, harder steel. Alloy steels are both: chromium, vanadium or nickel substitute for iron while carbon stays in the holes. In both kinds the foreign atoms distort the lattice and make it harder for planes to slip, which is why alloys are stronger than pure metals.

6.7Amorphous solids

An amorphous solid lacks long-range order, either because a normally crystalline material is full of defects or because its large molecules cannot line up. Amorphous solids soften over a range of temperature instead of melting sharply.

  • Microcrystalline solids have order only over short distances, broken by missing or misplaced atoms. Any crystalline substance cooled too quickly can form one. Hardened Portland cement (calcium silicate hydrate) has layers of silicate chains and calcium oxide broken by disordered regions and voids. Ceramics are microcrystalline regions cemented by glassy material, with ionic and covalent bonds: hard, strong in compression, heat- and chemical-resistant, but brittle and weak in tension. Their failures start at flaws, so processing must be tightly controlled.
  • Pseudo-solids (glasses, polymers, gels and thin films) consist of long, tangled structures and can flow very slowly under stress. They show a glass transition at Tg​, a reversible change from a hard, brittle glass to a rubbery or viscous state on heating.

Pure silica (quartz) melts at about 1700 °C and makes an excellent glass for ultraviolet optics, but it is hard to shape. Window glass is soda–lime glass: a covalent Si–O network with NaX+ and CaX2+ ions that break up the network, lowering the softening point. It has low thermal conductivity and fairly high expansion, so sudden heating or cooling sets up stresses that can shatter it. Annealing, holding the glass just below Tg​ and then cooling slowly, relieves these stresses. Organic polymers such as Bakelite, polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene and PTFE (Teflon) are also largely amorphous (Chapter 8).
