5.1Redox reactions

Electrochemistry deals with reactions in which electrons pass from one reactant to another: oxidation–reduction or redox reactions. Oxidation is the loss of electrons; reduction is the gain of electrons. The species that takes the electrons is the oxidising agent (it is itself reduced); the species that gives them up is the reducing agent (it is itself oxidised). When zinc burns, 2Zn(s)+OX2​(g)​2ZnO(s), zinc loses two electrons per atom and is the reducing agent; oxygen gains them and is the oxidising agent. Zinc in copper sulfate solution does the same with copper ions:

Zn(s)+CuX2+(aq)​ZnX2+(aq)+Cu(s)​Zn​ZnX2++2eX−CuX2++2eX−​Cu​​oxidationreduction​
Oxidation (top) and reduction (bottom) always happen together; the electrons lost must equal the electrons gained.

Oxidation states

The oxidation state (oxidation number) of an atom is the charge it would carry if all its bonds were ionic. A positive value counts electrons removed, a negative value electrons added. Oxidation raises the oxidation state and reduction lowers it. The rules:

  1. An atom in an element (Zn, OX2​, SX8​) has oxidation state 0.
  2. A monatomic ion has an oxidation state equal to its charge (FeX3+ is +3).
  3. The oxidation states in a neutral compound add up to 0.
  4. In a polyatomic ion they add up to the charge on the ion.
  5. Oxygen is −2 in almost all compounds, but −1 in peroxides such as HX2​OX2​ and NaX2​OX2​.
  6. Hydrogen is +1 in almost all compounds, but −1 in metal hydrides such as NaH.

Write the oxidation and reduction as separate half-reactions, multiply them so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained, and add. For copper in silver nitrate solution, copper loses two electrons but each silver ion takes only one, so the silver half-reaction is doubled:

5.2The galvanic cell

A galvanic (voltaic) cell, after Luigi Galvani and Alessandro Volta, takes electrical energy from a spontaneous redox reaction by making the electrons travel through an external wire. In the Daniell cell a zinc strip stands in zinc sulfate solution and a copper strip in copper sulfate solution. Zinc dissolves as ZnX2+, the electrons flow through the external circuit to the copper strip, and copper ions take them up and deposit as copper metal. The zinc electrode loses mass and the copper electrode gains it.

  • The electrode where oxidation takes place is the anode; in a galvanic cell it is the negative terminal.
  • The electrode where reduction takes place is the cathode, the positive terminal.
  • Electrons flow through the wire from anode to cathode (conventional current flows the other way).
  • The salt bridge, a tube of electrolyte such as KCl or NHX4​NOX3​, lets ions move between the two half-cells so that neither builds up a net charge, which would stop the current. Anions drift towards the anode and cations towards the cathode.

5.3Standard cell potential

The voltage between the two electrodes, the cell potential, depends on the species in the half-cells, their concentrations, the gas pressures and the temperature. To compare cells, a set of standard conditions is used: solutes at 1 M, gases at 1 atm, all substances in their standard states, and 298 K. The cell potential under these conditions is the standard cell potential Ecell∘​.

Only differences of potential can be measured, so one half-cell is chosen as the reference: the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), hydrogen gas at 1 atm bubbled over platinum in 1 M acid, assigned 0 V. It can act as an anode (HX2​(g)​2HX+(aq)+2eX−) or as a cathode (2HX+(aq)+2eX−​HX2​(g)). Paired with a copper half-cell, the SHE is the anode and the voltmeter reads +0.34 V, the standard potential of the copper half-cell:

CuX2+(aq)+2eX−​Cu(s)E∘=+0.34 V

Tabulated values are standard reduction potentials; the oxidation potential of the same half-cell has the opposite sign. The more positive E∘, the more readily the species is reduced. The standard cell potential is

Ecell∘​=Ecathode∘​−Eanode∘​
A positive E°cell means the reaction is spontaneous as written (ΔG° = −nFE°cell is negative).

5.4The Nernst equation

Standard potentials hold only at standard conditions. For other concentrations and temperatures the cell potential is given by the Nernst equation. For the reaction aA+bB⟶cC+dD,

Ecell​=Ecell∘​−nFRT​lnQ=Ecell∘​−nF2.303RT​logQEcell​=Ecell∘​−n0.0592​logQ  (298 K)
n = number of electrons transferred in the balanced reaction, Q = reaction quotient (solids are left out, as in an equilibrium constant), R = 8.314 J/(mol K), F = 96 485 C/mol. At 298 K, 2.303RT/F = 0.0592 V.

The Faraday constant F is the charge on one mole of electrons:

F=(1.602×10−19 C)×(6.022×1023 mol−1)=96485 C/mol

Use the concentration and temperature sliders under the 3D cell above to see the Nernst equation at work. As a cell discharges, Q grows and the voltage falls; when Q reaches the equilibrium constant, Ecell​=0 and the cell is flat.

5.5Electrolysis

In electrolysis an external current drives a redox reaction that would not go on its own. An electrolytic cell has an anode, a cathode and an electrolyte like a galvanic cell, but it turns electrical energy into chemical energy. The applied voltage must exceed the cell potential of the reverse reaction. The electrolyte must contain mobile ions, so electrolysis is done on molten salts or solutions. Its uses in engineering include extracting aluminium, refining copper, producing chlorine and sodium hydroxide, and electroplating.

Faraday's laws of electrolysis: (1) the mass of substance produced is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed, q=It; (2) for the same quantity of electricity, the masses of different substances are proportional to their equivalent weights (molar mass divided by the number of electrons per ion). Together:

m=nFItM​
m = mass deposited (g), I = current (A), t = time (s), M = molar mass (g/mol), n = electrons per ion, F = 96 485 C/mol.

In silver plating, a silver anode and the part to be plated (the cathode) stand in silver nitrate solution. The current dissolves the anode (Ag(s)​AgX+(aq)+eX−) and deposits silver on the cathode (AgX+(aq)+eX−​Ag(s)), so the concentration of silver ions stays roughly constant.

5.6Fuel cells

A fuel cell produces a continuous current by oxidising a fuel, usually hydrogen, with an oxidant, usually oxygen from the air. Unlike a battery it does not run down: it works for as long as fuel and air are supplied. At the anode hydrogen is split into protons and electrons. The protons pass through the electrolyte to the cathode; the electrons cannot, so they go round the external circuit, doing useful work on the way. At the cathode, oxygen, protons and electrons combine to form water, the only product.

2HX2​(g)​4HX++4eX−  (anode)OX2​(g)+4HX++4eX−​2HX2​O  (cathode)
Overall, 2H₂ + O₂ form 2H₂O with E°cell = 1.23 V; a working cell gives about 0.6 to 0.8 V, so stacks of cells are connected in series.

Proton exchange membrane fuel cell

Fuel cells are classified by their electrolyte:

  • Proton exchange membrane (PEMFC): a hydrated polymer membrane conducts protons but blocks electrons; runs at about 80 °C; used in vehicles.
  • Phosphoric acid (PAFC): liquid phosphoric acid carries the HX+ ions; used for stationary power.
  • Solid acid (SAFC): solid electrolytes such as CsHSOX4​ and CsHX2​POX4​.
  • Alkaline (AFC): concentrated KOH or NaOH, with porous carbon electrodes carrying a platinum catalyst; used on the Apollo spacecraft.

5.7Batteries

A battery is one or more galvanic cells with external terminals. Primary batteries cannot be recharged; secondary batteries can, because an external current reverses their half-reactions. A cell with a liquid electrolyte is a wet cell; with a paste or solid it is a dry cell. The electromotive force (emf) of a cell equals its cell potential, εcell​=Ecathode∘​−Eanode∘​.

Primary batteries

The Leclanché cell has a zinc anode and a cathode of manganese dioxide and carbon, with ammonium chloride electrolyte. Zinc is oxidised (Zn​ZnX2++2eX−) and ammonium ions are reduced (2NHX4​X++2eX−​2NHX3​+HX2​). The gases must be absorbed: ammonia forms Zn(NHX3​)X2​ClX2​ and hydrogen reduces MnOX2​ to MnX2​OX3​. The zinc–carbon dry cell uses the same chemistry with an ammonium chloride paste; the zinc can is the anode and a carbon rod collects current from the cathode mix. The zinc–alkaline cell (AAA, AA, C and D sizes) uses KOH electrolyte, purer and denser MnOX2​ and a more compact construction, giving three to five times the capacity of a zinc–carbon cell:

Zn(s)+2OHX−​ZnO(s)+HX2​O(l)+2eX−2MnOX2​(s)+HX2​O(l)+2eX−​MnX2​OX3​(s)+2OHX−

Alkaline cells should not be recharged in ordinary chargers (the case can rupture and leak corrosive KOH). Spent cells belong in battery collection points rather than household waste.

Secondary batteries

The lead–acid battery (Gaston Planté, 1859) has a lead anode, a lead dioxide cathode and sulfuric acid electrolyte, with insulating separators between the plates. One cell gives about 2.1 V, so a 12 V car battery has six cells in series (12.6 V when fully charged). On discharge both electrodes become coated with lead sulfate:

Pb(s)+HSOX4​X−​PbSOX4​(s)+HX++2eX−PbOX2​(s)+HSOX4​X−+3HX++2eX−​PbSOX4​(s)+2HX2​O(l)
On charging both reactions run backwards. Discharge uses up sulfuric acid, so the acid density is a measure of the state of charge.

Overcharging electrolyses the water into hydrogen and oxygen, so older batteries had filler caps for topping up. Over time the sulfate flakes off and the battery can no longer be recharged. Lead is toxic, so lead–acid batteries must be recycled; in practice about 99 % of them are.

The nickel–cadmium (NiCd) cell has a cadmium anode, a nickel oxide hydroxide cathode and KOH electrolyte, and gives a steady 1.2 V. On discharge cadmium is oxidised to Cd(OH)X2​ and nickel is reduced from +3 to +2 (NiO(OH) to Ni(OH)X2​). Cadmium is toxic, so NiCd cells have largely been replaced by nickel–metal hydride (NiMH) cells, in which the anode is a hydrogen-absorbing alloy (types ABX5​, with A a mix of rare earths and B nickel, cobalt, manganese or aluminium, and ABX2​, with A titanium or vanadium and B zirconium or nickel) and the cathode is nickel oxide hydroxide. On discharge:

MH(s)+OHX−​M(s)+HX2​O(l)+eX−  (anode)NiO(OH)(s)+HX2​O(l)+eX−​Ni(OH)X2​(s)+OHX−  (cathode)

The lithium-ion battery (first sold in 1991) powers phones, laptops, solar storage and electric vehicles. Lithium reacts with water, so the electrolyte is a lithium salt (such as LiPFX6​) in an organic solvent, with a porous separator between the electrodes. Both electrodes are layered structures, and lithium ions move between the layers, a process called intercalation. On discharge lithium ions leave the graphite anode, cross the electrolyte and enter the cobalt oxide cathode, where cobalt is reduced from +4 to +3:

LiCX6​(s)​CX6​(s)+LiX++eX−  (anode)CoOX2​(s)+LiX++eX−​LiCoOX2​(s)  (cathode)

Lithium ions moving between the electrodes

Lithium-ion cells typically last 500 to 1500 full cycles; cells with lithium titanate anodes exceed 4000. Large packs combine cells in series to raise the voltage and in parallel to raise the capacity. Lithium-ion cells must not go into household waste: damaged cells can catch fire in bins and refuse trucks, and the cobalt, nickel, lithium and copper they contain are valuable, so they are collected and recycled.
