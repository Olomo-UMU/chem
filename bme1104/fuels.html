9.1Fuels and calorific value

Fuels are substances that burn readily and give out large amounts of heat for domestic and industrial use. Most have carbon as their main constituent. Fossil fuels (coal, peat, crude oil and natural gas) formed from organic matter over millions of years and are not renewable. Fuels are used mainly by combustion, the oxidation of their carbon and hydrogen to carbon dioxide and water; the difference in energy between the reactants and the products is released as heat.

Calorific value

The heat obtainable from a fuel is its calorific value (CV), in kJ/kg or kJ/m³ (also kcal/kg, kcal/m³ or Btu/lb; 1 kcal = 4.187 kJ).

  • The gross or higher calorific value (GCV, HCV) is the heat released when a unit amount of fuel burns completely in oxygen, the fuel and products starting and ending at 25 °C, with the water formed condensed to liquid.
  • The net or lower calorific value (NCV, LCV) is the same but with the water left as vapour. It is lower by the latent heat of the water, 587 kcal/kg (2457 kJ/kg). In boilers and engines the water leaves as steam in the exhaust, so the net value is what is actually available.

Each kilogram of hydrogen burns to 9 kg of water (2 g of H give 18 g of water), so for a fuel with H % hydrogen:

NCV=GCV−1009H​×587=GCV−0.09H×587  kcal/kg
H = percentage of hydrogen by mass; 0.09H kg of water per kg of fuel.

9.2Measuring calorific value

The bomb calorimeter

The bomb calorimeter measures the calorific value of solid and liquid fuels. A strong stainless-steel vessel, the bomb, has a lid screwed down airtight, carrying an oxygen valve and two electrodes. A weighed pellet of fuel sits in a nickel crucible, touched by a fine fuse wire (magnesium or nickel–chromium) between the electrodes. The bomb is filled with oxygen at about 25 atm and stands in a copper calorimeter holding a known mass of water, with a stirrer and a Beckmann thermometer that reads to 0.01 °C. An air gap and an outer water jacket cut heat losses.

The initial temperature t1​ is recorded, the fuel is ignited electrically, and the highest temperature t2​ is noted. The heat released by the fuel is taken up by the water and the calorimeter:

GCV=m(W+w)(t2​−t1​)​
m = mass of fuel (g), W = mass of water (g), w = water equivalent of the calorimeter (g): the mass of water that would take up the same heat as the bomb, vessel and stirrer. The result is in cal/g, equal to kcal/kg.

The water equivalent is found by burning a standard of known calorific value: benzoic acid (6325 kcal/kg) or naphthalene (9688 kcal/kg). For accurate work three corrections are applied: the fuse wire correction (heat from the wire), the acid correction (heat from forming sulfuric and nitric acids from sulfur and nitrogen in the fuel) and the cooling correction (the rate of cooling after the peak, in °C per minute, multiplied by the time taken to reach the peak, added to t2​−t1​):

GCV=m(W+w)(t2​−t1​+tc​)−(acid+fuse)​

Calorific value from composition: Dulong's formula

The calorific value can be estimated from the ultimate analysis (the percentages of C, H, O and S), using the heats of combustion of carbon (8080 kcal/kg), hydrogen (34 500 kcal/kg) and sulfur (2240 kcal/kg). Oxygen in the fuel is assumed to be combined already with hydrogen as water ("fixed" hydrogen, one eighth of the oxygen by mass), so only the remaining available hydrogen H−O/8 burns:

GCV=1001​[8080C+34500(H−8O​)+2240S] kcal/kg
C, H, O, S are percentages by mass.

Dulong's formula

The Boys gas calorimeter

The Boys calorimeter measures the calorific value of gaseous (and volatile liquid) fuels. A known volume V of gas burns at a steady rate in a burner inside a chimney wound with copper coils, through which cooling water flows at a constant rate. Thermometers read the inlet and outlet water temperatures t1​ and t2​, and the steam condensed from the flue gases (mass m) is collected. If W is the mass of water that flows while V is burnt,

GCV=VW(t2​−t1​)​NCV=GCV−Vm×587​  kcal/m3

Air for combustion

The air needed to burn a fuel follows from the balanced combustion equations. For gases, volumes react in the same ratio as moles. Air contains 21 % oxygen by volume (23 % by mass), so the air needed is the oxygen needed × 100/21. In practice excess air is supplied to make sure all the fuel burns; too much wastes heat in the flue gas.

9.3Solid fuels

Wood

Wood, from the trunks and branches of trees, holds about 15 % moisture after air drying. It is mainly lignocellulose (cellulose with resins and proteins). On a moisture-free basis it averages C 55 %, H 6 %, O 43 % and ash 1 %, with a calorific value of 4000 to 6400 Btu/lb (2200 to 3600 kcal/kg). It is chiefly a domestic fuel; charcoal, made by heating wood without air, burns hotter and cleaner.

Coal

Coal formed from prehistoric plants through coalification over millions of years: wood, then peat, lignite, bituminous coal and finally anthracite. Along this series the moisture, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur and volatile matter fall, while the carbon content, calorific value and hardness rise.

Analysis of coal

Proximate analysis finds the moisture, volatile matter, ash and fixed carbon of air-dried coal by heating and weighing:

  1. Moisture: 1 g of powdered coal is heated at 105 to 110 °C for an hour; the loss in mass is the moisture.
  2. Volatile matter: the dried sample is heated in a covered crucible at 950 ± 20 °C for 7 minutes; the loss is the volatile matter. (The notes print 250 °C; the standard test is at 950 °C.)
  3. Ash: the residue is heated in an open crucible at 700 ± 50 °C until its mass is constant; what remains is ash.
  4. Fixed carbon is what is left: 100 minus the percentages of moisture, volatile matter and ash.
%moisture, volatile matter or ash=mass of coal takenloss (or residue) in mass​×100

Moisture lowers the calorific value (its evaporation takes heat). Much of the volatile matter escapes unburnt as smoke, so less is better for a boiler fuel, although some volatile matter is needed for coking. Ash adds nothing, fouls furnaces and must be disposed of. The higher the fixed carbon, the higher the calorific value and the better the coal.

Ultimate analysis finds the percentages of C, H, N, S and O. For carbon and hydrogen, 1 to 2 g of coal is burnt in a stream of oxygen; the carbon dioxide is absorbed in a weighed tube of KOH and the water in a weighed tube of anhydrous CaClX2​:
