3.1The octet rule and ions

Bonding is the combination of atoms to form a molecule or a solid. A chemical bond is the force of attraction that holds the atoms together. Atoms combine so that each reaches a stable electron arrangement, usually that of a noble gas. The main types of bond are the ionic (electrovalent) bond, the covalent bond and the dative (coordinate) bond; metals are held together by the metallic bond (Chapter 6).

The octet rule: atoms tend to gain, lose or share electrons until they have eight electrons in the outer shell, the arrangement ns2np6 of the noble gases. It works best for the main-group elements of periods 2 and 3. Hydrogen, helium and lithium follow the duplet rule instead, since their only shell (n = 1) holds two electrons. Some atoms are stable with fewer than eight (boron in BFX3​) and some with more, an expanded octet (sulfur in SFX6​, phosphorus in PClX5​).

  • Sodium, 1s22s22p63s1, loses its single 3s electron to become NaX+ with the configuration of neon.
  • Chlorine, 1s22s22p63s23p5, gains one electron to become ClX− with the configuration of argon.
  • Oxygen (six valence electrons) shares one electron with each of two hydrogen atoms in HX2​O; carbon (four) shares with four hydrogens in CHX4​.

Ions and their sizes

Noble gases already have a closed shell and do not form ions. Ions are less reactive than their parent atoms because their configurations are more stable. A cation has fewer electrons than protons, so the remaining electrons are pulled in and the cation is smaller than its atom. An anion has more electrons than protons, so it is larger than its atom. In general:

  • cations are smaller than their parent atoms;
  • anions are larger than their parent atoms;
  • anions are generally larger than cations;
  • a higher charge gives a smaller ion (FeX3+ 64 pm, FeX2+ 77 pm);
  • ionic radius increases down a group;
  • ionic radius generally decreases across a period for the same charge.

3.2Types of bond

The ionic bond

An ionic bond forms between dissimilar atoms when one gives up one or more electrons (forming a cation) and the other takes them (forming an anion). The ions are held by electrostatic attraction. The number of electrons an atom gains or loses is its electrovalence. The potential energy of two ions with charges q1​ and q2​ at a distance d follows from Coulomb's law:

E=kdq1​q2​​
k = 8.988 × 10⁹ N m²/C². For a cation and an anion, q₁q₂ is negative, so E is negative and the ions attract.

As the ions approach, the energy falls. When their electron clouds start to overlap a strong repulsion sets in, so the total energy passes through a minimum. The depth of the minimum measures the bond strength; the distance at the minimum is the bond length, about the sum of the two ionic radii, d=r+​+r−​. Hence:

  • the smaller the ions, the shorter the bond and the stronger it is;
  • the larger the charges, the stronger the bond.

Energy of an ion pair against distance

Ionic bonding is favoured by a low ionisation energy of the metal (the cation forms easily), a high electron affinity of the non-metal (the anion forms easily) and a strong attraction between the ions (small, highly charged ions).

The covalent bond

A covalent bond forms when two atoms, both short of a stable configuration, share a pair of electrons, as in H–H or H–Cl. Describing bonds as shared pairs held between two atoms is valence bond theory. A covalent molecule has bond pairs (shared) and lone pairs (not shared, but counted towards the octet). Sharing one pair gives a single bond, two pairs a double bond and three pairs a triple bond. Multiple bonds are shorter and stronger:

Bond polarity

When both atoms have the same electronegativity the pair is shared equally: a nonpolar covalent bond. When they differ, the pair is pulled towards the more electronegative atom, which gains a partial negative charge δ−, while the other becomes δ+. This is a polar covalent bond, or dipole. Its strength is the dipole moment μ = δ d, in coulomb metres (C m), often quoted in debye (1 D = 3.336 × 10−30 C m). A rule of thumb from the electronegativity difference ΔEN:

  • ΔEN of 0.4 or less: nonpolar covalent;
  • ΔEN between 0.4 and about 2: polar covalent;
  • ΔEN above about 2: ionic.

Who gets the electrons?

The dative (coordinate) bond

In a dative bond both electrons of the shared pair come from one atom, the donor, which has a lone pair. The other atom, the acceptor, is short of electrons. The bond is drawn as an arrow from donor to acceptor. When ammonia meets a hydrogen ion, nitrogen donates its lone pair:

HX3​N:+HX+​[NHX4​]X+
Once formed, the dative N–H bond is identical to the other three: the ammonium ion is a perfect tetrahedron.

Mixed ionic and covalent bonding

Polyatomic ions are groups of atoms held by covalent bonds that, as a group, have gained or lost electrons. They behave as single units in ionic compounds: in CaCOX3​ the carbonate ion COX3​X2− is covalent inside but bonded ionically to CaX2+. Others are ammonium NHX4​X+, hydroxide OHX−, nitrate NOX3​X−, sulfate SOX4​X2− and phosphate POX4​X3−.

3.3Molecular orbitals

In a covalent bond the shared electrons no longer sit in atomic orbitals but in molecular orbitals formed by the overlap of atomic orbitals. The number of molecular orbitals equals the number of atomic orbitals that combine. When two 1s orbitals overlap in phase they form a bonding orbital, lower in energy, with more electron density between the nuclei. Out of phase they form an antibonding orbital (marked *), higher in energy, with a node between the nuclei.

Electrons fill molecular orbitals by the same Aufbau, Pauli and Hund rules as atomic orbitals. Orbitals along the bond axis are σ (sigma) orbitals; those formed by side-on overlap of p orbitals are π (pi) orbitals. The bond order tells whether the molecule exists and how strong the bond is:

bond order=21​(bonding electrons−antibonding electrons)
Bond order 0 means no bond (He₂ does not exist). Unpaired electrons make the molecule paramagnetic (attracted into a magnetic field).

3.4Molecular geometry

The shape of a molecule decides its reactivity, polarity, physical state, melting and boiling points. Shape depends on bond lengths, bond angles and where the electron pairs sit. Electron pairs repel each other, so the electron groups around a central atom (each bond, single or multiple, counts as one group, and so does each lone pair) spread as far apart as possible. This is the valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) model.

A lone pair also repels more strongly than a bond pair, so it squeezes the bond angles: 109.5° in CHX4​, 107° in NHX3​ (one lone pair) and 104.5° in HX2​O (two).

Molecular polarity

A molecule is nonpolar if its terminal atoms are all the same and are arranged symmetrically around the central atom; otherwise it is polar. The polarity is the vector sum of the bond dipoles. In COX2​ both C=O bonds are polar, but they point in exactly opposite directions and cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar. In SOX2​ the lone pair on sulfur bends the molecule, the bond dipoles no longer cancel, and the molecule is polar with its negative end at the oxygens.

3.5Intermolecular forces

Intermolecular forces, also called van der Waals forces, act between molecules. They are weak compared with the covalent or ionic bonds inside molecules, but they decide the boiling and freezing points, vapour pressure, viscosity, surface tension and solubility, and so whether a substance is a solid, liquid or gas at a given temperature.

  • London dispersion forces act between all molecules, even nonpolar ones, through brief random dipoles in the electron clouds. They grow with the size of the molecule (more electrons).
  • Dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules: the δ+ end of one molecule attracts the δ− end of its neighbour, as in HCl.
  • Hydrogen bonds are the strongest dipole–dipole forces. They form when hydrogen is bonded to F, O or N, which are small and very electronegative (3.98, 3.44 and 3.04 against 2.20 for H). The hydrogen is left almost a bare proton and is attracted to a lone pair on a neighbouring F, O or N. Hydrogen bonds hold the two strands of DNA together and fold proteins into their shapes.
