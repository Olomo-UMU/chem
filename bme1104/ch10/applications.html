10.2

Bonding and structure decide material choice

Chapters 2, 3 and 6 at work: why shafts are steel, cutting tips are ceramic and gears are nylon.

From bond type to engineering property

Bond typeTypical propertiesMechanical engineering use
Metallic (steel, Al, Cu)Ductile, tough, conductive, weldableShafts, frames, pressure vessels, heat exchangers
Ionic and covalent ceramics (Al₂O₃, ZrO₂, SiC)Hard, wear and heat resistant, brittleTool inserts, grinding wheels, pump seals, furnace linings
Covalent network (diamond, carbides)Hardest materials knownDrill bits, cutting tools, hard coatings
Polymer chains and intermolecular forcesLight, corrosion resistant, low friction, soften when hotGears, bearings, seals, pipes, housings

Key idea

Delocalised electrons let metal atoms slide past each other without breaking bonds, so metals bend before they break. Fixed, directional bonds in ceramics cannot do this, so they crack instead.

Thermal barrier coatings on turbine blades

Hot combustion gas, above the alloy's melting point

Yttria-stabilised zirconia topcoat: ionic ceramic, very low thermal conductivity

Thermally grown oxide, Al₂O₃ (a few micrometres)

Metallic bond coat (NiCoCrAlY or Pt-aluminide)

Nickel superalloy blade, internally air-cooled

Why it works

  • The ceramic topcoat insulates: the metal runs roughly 100 °C or more cooler
  • The bond coat forms a thin, adherent alumina layer that blocks oxygen
  • Coating life is set by chemistry: as the oxide thickens, stresses build and the ceramic spalls off
  • Source: Padture et al. (2002)

Carbon in iron

Small carbon atoms sit in the gaps between iron atoms and distort the lattice, blocking slip. Carbon content and heat treatment therefore control the strength of steel.

Chromium makes steel stainless

At about 10.5% Cr or more, chromium forms a thin, self-healing Cr₂O₃ film. Chlorides can break it locally, so stainless steel can still pit in salty water.

Water chemistry in boilers and cooling systems

Chapter 4 (equilibrium, Ksp, pH) inside every steam boiler in sugar factories, breweries, textile mills and dairies.

Scale is Le Chatelier's principle inside a boiler tube

Ca(HCOX3​)X2​(aq)​CaCOX3​(s)+COX2​(g)+HX2​O(l)Ksp​(CaCOX3​)=[CaX2+][COX3​X2−]=3.3×10−9  at 25∘C

What happens

  • Heating drives off CO₂, so the equilibrium shifts right
  • CaCO₃ is less soluble when hot
  • Scale grows on the hottest surfaces first: boiler tubes, heat exchangers, engine water jackets

Where it matters in Uganda

  • Sugar factories raising steam from bagasse
  • Breweries, dairies and textile mills
  • Generator and vehicle cooling systems filled with hard borehole water

Worked example: is this cooling water scale-forming?

Problem

Cooling tower water has pH 7.8 at 35 °C, total dissolved solids 500 mg/L, calcium hardness 200 mg/L and alkalinity 150 mg/L (both as CaCO₃).

Calculate the Langelier saturation index and decide whether the water will scale.

  1. A = (log 500 − 1)/10 = 0.170
  2. B = −13.12 log(308) + 34.55 = 1.89
  3. C = log 200 − 0.4 = 1.90;  D = log 150 = 2.18
  4. pHₛ = (9.3 + 0.170 + 1.89) − (1.90 + 2.18) = 7.28
  5. LSI = 7.8 − 7.28 = +0.52
LSI = +0.52, so the water is scale-forming. Dose a scale inhibitor, or lower pH or alkalinity, before it reaches hot surfaces.

How industry treats boiler and cooling water

Softening

Ion exchange swaps Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ for Na⁺, or removes all ions for high-pressure boilers; reverse osmosis is growing

Deaeration

Heating plus an oxygen scavenger such as sodium sulfite removes dissolved O₂, which causes pitting

pH control

Boiler water kept alkaline (about pH 9 to 11) so a protective magnetite (Fe₃O₄) film forms

Blowdown

Draining concentrated boiler water, controlled by conductivity (Chapter 10B)

Cooling-tower dosing

Scale and corrosion inhibitors and biocides; LSI kept near zero (MacAdam & Parsons, 2004)

Monitoring

Daily hardness, pH, conductivity and iron tests in the boiler house (Flynn, 2009)

Corrosion and its control

Chapter 5 running on its own: every rusting surface is a galvanic cell.

Corrosion is a galvanic cell

​Anode:Cathode:Rust:​​Fe​FeX2++2eX−OX2​+2HX2​O+4eX−​4OHX−FeX2++2OHX−​Fe(OH)X2​OX2​​FeX2​OX3​⋅xHX2​O​

Every protection method removes one of the four

Coatings remove the electrolyte; insulating joints break the metallic path; cathodic protection makes the structure a cathode; deaeration removes the oxygen.

A corrosion cell needs

  • An anode (metal oxidised)
  • A cathode (O₂ or H⁺ reduced)
  • An electrolyte (water, soil, condensate)
  • A metallic path for electrons

Galvanic cell simulation

Three lines of defence against corrosion

1 Coatings

  • Paint, epoxy and polymer coatings keep the electrolyte away
  • Galvanised sheets (mabati): zinc also protects sacrificially where scratched
  • Aluminium–zinc coatings add a tough oxide film

2 Cathodic protection

  • Make the whole structure the cathode
  • Sacrificial anodes of Zn, Al or Mg: hulls, tanks, water heaters
  • Impressed current from a rectifier: pipelines, jetties, reinforced concrete

3 Inhibitors

  • Small amounts adsorb on the metal and slow the anode or cathode reaction
  • Cooling water, fuel and oil systems, acid cleaning
  • Research: non-toxic inhibitors from plant extracts (Verma et al., 2018)

Worked example: life of a sacrificial zinc anode

Problem

A zinc anode bolted to a steel water tank delivers an average protection current of 0.50 A.

How much zinc is consumed in one year? If the anode weighs 6 kg and 85% can be used, how long will it last?

Zn: M = 65.38 g/mol, n = 2.

m=nFεItM​,δ=ρAm​=nFρεitM​
  1. Q = I t = 0.50 × 3.154 × 10⁷ = 1.58 × 10⁷ C
  2. Moles Zn = Q/(nF) = 1.58 × 10⁷ / (2 × 96 485) = 81.7 mol
  3. Mass = 81.7 × 65.38 = 5.34 kg per year
  4. Usable zinc = 0.85 × 6 = 5.1 kg
About 5.3 kg of zinc a year: the anode lasts about 0.95 years. Replace it at each annual inspection.

Worked example: how long to nickel-plate a part?

Problem

A steel part of area 2 dm² needs a 25 μm nickel coating. The bath runs at 4 A/dm² with 95% current efficiency.

Ni: M = 58.69 g/mol, n = 2, ρ = 8.90 g/cm³.

How long must it be plated, and how much nickel is used?

  1. m = δAρ = (25 × 10⁻⁴ cm)(200 cm²)(8.90 g/cm³) = 4.45 g
  2. I = iA = 4 × 2 = 8 A
  3. t = m n F / (ε I M) = 4.45 × 2 × 96 485 / (0.95 × 8 × 58.69)
  4. t = 1.93 × 10³ s
About 32 minutes, depositing 4.45 g of nickel.

Batteries and fuel cells

Section 5.7 in vehicles, telecom masts, solar systems and electric buses.

Lead–acid battery chemistry sets life and failure

​(−)  Pb+HSOX4​X−​PbSOX4​+HX++2eX−(+)  PbOX2​+HSOX4​X−+3HX++2eX−​PbSOX4​+2HX2​OOverall:  Pb+PbOX2​+2HX2​SOX4​dischargecharge​2PbSOX4​+2HX2​O​

Discharge uses up the acid

Both plates turn to PbSO₄ and H₂SO₄ is consumed, so the electrolyte's specific gravity falls with the state of charge. A hydrometer reading is a chemical measurement of charge.

Chemical failure modes

  • Sulfation: PbSO₄ crystals coarsen when left discharged
  • Grid corrosion of the positive plate: worse with high voltage and heat
  • Water loss by electrolysis on overcharge
  • Over 95% are recycled where collection exists (May et al., 2018)

Lithium-ion batteries and fuel cells

Lithium-ion

  • Li⁺ shuttles between a graphite anode and an oxide or phosphate cathode
  • About 150 to 250 Wh/kg against 30 to 40 for lead–acid (Goodenough & Park, 2013)
  • Keep cells near 15 to 35 °C; overheating can trigger thermal runaway

Calorific value of industrial fuels

Worked example: hydrogen and GCV of a coal

Problem (Chapter 9, Example 1)

A coal contains C = 90%, O = 3%, S = 0.5%, N = 0.5% and ash = 2.5%. Its net calorific value is 8,490.5 kcal/kg.

Calculate the percentage of hydrogen and the gross calorific value.

  1. GCV = 0.01[8080(90) + 34 500(H − 0.375) + 2240(0.5)] = 7153.8 + 345H
  2. NCV = GCV − 0.09H × 587 = 7153.8 + 292.2H
  3. 8490.5 = 7153.8 + 292.2H, so H = 4.58%
  4. GCV = 7153.8 + 345 × 4.58 = 8732 kcal/kg
H = 4.58% and GCV = 8,732 kcal/kg (36.6 MJ/kg). The composition then sums to about 101%, so the data are slightly inconsistent.

Knock, octane number and ethanol blending

Octane and knock (Chapter 7)

  • Knock: unburned mixture ignites on its own ahead of the flame
  • Branched iso-octane resists knock (octane 100); straight-chain heptane knocks (0)
  • Ethanol: RON about 108, contains oxygen, but LHV 26.8 MJ/kg and stoichiometric AFR 9.0 (Masum et al., 2015)
  • Ethanol absorbs water and attacks some rubbers: fuel-system materials must be compatible

Uganda

Blending is moving from E1 to E3, with the 5% (E5) target expected within months ("Uganda Set to Hit," 2026).

Choosing a seal material for a fluid

A rubber chemically similar to the fluid absorbs it and swells. Polar nitrile resists mineral oil; EPDM resists water and steam but not oil (Parker Hannifin Corporation, 2021).

Recycling plastics and metals

Plastics

  • Thermoplastics can be remelted only if sorted by type: hence the resin codes (Section 8.4)
  • Pyrolysis: heating mixed plastics without oxygen at 400 to 600 °C cracks chains into an oil comparable to commercial fuel (Anuar Sharuddin et al., 2016)
  • PVC is avoided: it releases corrosive HCl

Metals

  • Fe, Si and Cu build up in recycled aluminium and are hard to remove (Gaustad et al., 2012)
  • Scrap must be sorted by alloy, increasingly by laser spectroscopy (Chapter 10B)
  • Lead–acid batteries: over 95% recycled where collection exists
