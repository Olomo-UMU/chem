Measurement, calibration and spectrophotometry

How a signal becomes a trustworthy number, using iron in boiler water as the example.

Four words every lab report depends on

Accuracy

Closeness to the true value. Its opposite is bias: a systematic error such as an uncalibrated balance.

Precision

Closeness of repeated readings to each other. A precise instrument can still be inaccurate.

Calibration

Measuring standards of known value to convert the instrument's signal into a result.

Uncertainty and traceability

Report a range (2.43 ± 0.05 mg/L, 95%) and use standards traceable to national references.

UV–visible spectrophotometry and the Beer–Lambert law

A=−log10​I0​I​=εbc
ε molar absorptivity, b path length (1 cm), c concentration

How it works

  • A lamp and monochromator select one wavelength
  • Light passes through the sample in a cuvette
  • A detector compares transmitted intensity I with a blank I₀
  • Linear only over a limited range: dilute samples above A = 1

Iron in boiler water

  • Fe²⁺ reacts with 1,10-phenanthroline to give an orange-red complex
  • Absorbance is measured at 510 nm; ε = 11,100 L/(mol cm) (APHA, 2017)
  • Rising iron in condensate or feedwater means corrosion upstream, so power stations and sugar factories trend it daily

pH and conductivity

The Nernst equation (Section 5.4) as an instrument, and conductivity control of steam boilers.

The pH meter is a galvanic cell

E=E0​−kF2.303RT​(pH−7),F2.303RT​=59.16 mV per pH  (25∘C)

The cell

  • Glass electrode: membrane potential depends on H⁺ activity
  • Ag/AgCl reference electrode: constant potential

Calibration

  • Two or three buffers: pH 4.01, 7.00, 10.01
  • Fixes the offset (E at pH 7) and the slope

Electrode health

  • Slope below about 95%: clean or replace
  • Offset beyond about ±30 mV: check the reference junction

Why the pH meter measures temperature

Compensation corrects the electrode slope, not the real change of the sample's pH with temperature.

Worked example: conductivity controls boiler blowdown

Problem

A sugar-factory boiler produces 20 t/h of steam at 10 bar. Feedwater conductivity is 250 μS/cm; boiler water must stay below 3,500 μS/cm.

Find the cycles of concentration, the blowdown rate and the heat lost (boiler water 763 kJ/kg, feedwater at 105 °C 440 kJ/kg).

COC=κfeed​κboiler​​,B=COC−1S​
  1. COC = 3500 / 250 = 14
  2. Blowdown B = S/(COC − 1) = 20/13 = 1.54 t/h
  3. Feedwater = 20 + 1.54 = 21.54 t/h
  4. Heat lost = 1540 kg/h × (763 − 440) kJ/kg ÷ 3600 = 138 kW
Blowdown 1.54 t/h, losing about 138 kW of heat. Halving feedwater conductivity (better treatment) cuts this to about 66 kW (Flynn, 2009).

Worked example: hardness of boiler make-up water

Problem

A 50.0 mL water sample, buffered to pH 10 with Eriochrome Black T, needs 9.60 mL of 0.0100 mol/L EDTA to turn from wine red to blue.

Find the total hardness as mg/L CaCO₃. Is this water fit for a boiler without softening?

Hardness=Vsample​VEDTA​cEDTA​×100090​  mg/L as CaCO3​
  1. EDTA reacts 1 : 1 with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺: 9.60 × 10⁻³ L × 0.0100 M = 9.60 × 10⁻⁵ mol
  2. As CaCO₃: 9.60 × 10⁻⁵ × 100 090 mg/mol = 9.61 mg
  3. Hardness = 9.61 mg ÷ 0.0500 L = 192 mg/L
192 mg/L as CaCO₃: very hard. It must be softened or demineralised; boiler feedwater hardness should be close to zero (APHA, 2017).

Worked example: iron in an ore by redox titration

Problem

A 0.2500 g sample of iron ore is dissolved and all the iron reduced to Fe²⁺. Titration needs 22.40 mL of 0.02000 mol/L KMnO₄.

Calculate the percentage of iron. Permanganate is its own indicator: the first excess drop turns the solution pink.

MnOX4​X−+5FeX2++8HX+​MnX2++5FeX3++4HX2​O
  1. Moles MnO₄⁻ = 22.40 × 10⁻³ × 0.02000 = 4.480 × 10⁻⁴ mol
  2. Moles Fe²⁺ = 5 × 4.480 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.240 × 10⁻³ mol
  3. Mass Fe = 2.240 × 10⁻³ × 55.845 = 0.1251 g
  4. % Fe = 0.1251 / 0.2500 × 100
Iron content = 50.0%. The same titration checks iron in water-treatment chemicals and ores.

Cathodic protection survey of a buried pipeline

Eoff​≤−850 mV vs CSE
instant-off potential, current interrupted (NACE International, 2013; ISO, 2015)

Reading the survey

  • Readings are taken at test posts about every kilometre
  • A poor coating draws more current, so protection decays faster with distance
  • Fix: raise the rectifier output, add a rectifier, or repair the coating

Too negative is also harmful

Below about −1,200 mV the steel makes hydrogen: coatings disbond and high-strength steels can be embrittled.

Pipeline potential survey

Corrosion monitoring in operating plants

LPR probes

Apply ±10 to 20 mV to small electrodes; the corrosion current follows from the polarisation resistance in minutes.

Electrical resistance probes

A thin metal element's resistance rises as it corrodes away; works in any fluid, even non-conducting.

Coupons

Weighed metal strips exposed for weeks, then cleaned and reweighed: mass loss gives the average rate.

icorr​=Rp​B​,B=2.303(βa​+βc​)βa​βc​​
Stern and Geary (1957): βa, βc are the Tafel slopes; B is about 26 mV for actively corroding steel

Calorimetry and fuel analysis

Sections 9.1.2 and 9.2.2 as laboratory procedures: the bomb calorimeter, proximate and ultimate analysis.

Bomb calorimeter procedure (ASTM D240, ISO 1928)

  1. Calibrate: burn about 1 g of certified benzoic acid (6,325 kcal/kg) to find the water equivalent w
  2. Weigh 0.8 to 1 g of fuel into the crucible, attach the fuse wire, add 1 mL of water, fill with O₂ to 25 to 30 atm
  3. Immerse the bomb, stir, and record the temperature every minute until it is steady
  4. Fire; record the rise to the maximum and the cooling afterwards
  5. Correct: add the cooling correction to Δt; subtract fuse-wire and acid heat (titrate the bomb washings)
HCV=m(W+w)(t2​−t1​+tcool​)−(acid+fuse)​  cal/g
w=ΔtBA​mBA​×6325+fuse​−W
calibration with benzoic acid (cal/g)

The bomb measures the gross value: the water formed condenses inside. Subtract 0.09 H × 587 for the net value.

Worked example: bomb calorimeter (Chapter 9, Example 2)

Problem

Burning 0.83 g of a solid fuel raised the temperature of 3,500 g of water from 26.5 °C to 29.2 °C. Water equivalent 385 g; latent heat of steam 587 cal/g; the fuel contains 0.7% H.

Calculate the HCV and NCV.

  1. Heat absorbed = (W + w)(t₂ − t₁) = (3500 + 385)(2.7) = 10 489.5 cal
  2. HCV = 10 489.5 / 0.83 = 12 638 cal/g
  3. NCV = HCV − 0.09 × 0.7 × 587 = 12 638 − 37.0
  4. NCV = 12 601 cal/g
HCV = 12,638 cal/g and NCV = 12,601 cal/g (52.9 and 52.8 MJ/kg).

Sense check: real coal is 25 to 35 MJ/kg, so these textbook data are idealised.

Worked example: proximate analysis from crucible weighings

Problem (ASTM D3172)

Crucible + lid20.0000 g
With sample21.0000 g
1 h at 105–110 °C20.9450 g
7 min at 950 °C, lid on20.6240 g
Ashed at 750 °C, lid off20.1050 g

Find M, VM, ash and FC.

  1. Moisture = (21.0000 − 20.9450)/1.0000 × 100 = 5.50%
  2. Volatile matter = (20.9450 − 20.6240)/1.0000 × 100 = 32.10%
  3. Ash = (20.1050 − 20.0000)/1.0000 × 100 = 10.50%
  4. Fixed carbon = 100 − (5.50 + 32.10 + 10.50) = 51.90%
M 5.5%, VM 32.1%, ash 10.5%, FC 51.9%. Fuel ratio FC/VM = 1.6, typical of a bituminous coal.

Volatile matter is found at 950 ± 20 °C (ASTM D3175), not 250 °C as printed in the notes.

Classical (Section 9.2.2)

Burn 1 to 2 g in O₂; weigh the CO₂ absorbed in KOH and the water absorbed in CaCl₂. Slow, but needs only a balance and glassware.

Modern CHNS analyser

A few milligrams burn at about 1,000 °C; gas chromatography separates the gases and a thermal conductivity detector measures them. C, H, N and S in minutes.

Identifying metals and polymers

Positive material identification with XRF and LIBS; FTIR, AAS/ICP and XRD.

Identifying alloys in seconds with XRF and LIBS

X-ray fluorescence (XRF)

X-rays excite atoms and each element re-emits X-rays at its own energies. A handheld gun names an alloy grade in seconds without damage, but cannot see carbon.

Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS)

A laser vaporises a tiny spot; the plasma's light identifies elements, including light ones (C, Mg, Al). Scrap sorters handle up to about 100 pieces per second (Noll et al., 2018).

Spectroscopy for oils, polymers and crystal structure

FTIR

Identifies chemical bonds from infrared absorption. Polymers in seconds (C=O near 1,712 cm⁻¹ marks PET); used oil checked for oxidation, water, glycol and soot.

AAS and ICP

Metals at mg/kg levels. Rising Fe, Cu, Pb or Cr in used oil shows which part wears; Si shows dirt; Na and K show a coolant leak (Wakiru et al., 2019).

X-ray diffraction

Plane spacing identifies crystal phases (Chapter 6): retained austenite, corrosion products, cement phases.

Worked example: melt flow index of a polypropylene

Problem

In a melt flow test at 230 °C under 2.16 kg, extrudate is cut off every 30 s. Five cut-offs weigh 0.62, 0.60, 0.63, 0.61 and 0.62 g.

Calculate the melt flow index. Does the grade suit injection moulding or pipe extrusion?

MFI=tcut​mˉ×600​  g/10min
  1. Mean cut-off mass = (0.62 + 0.60 + 0.63 + 0.61 + 0.62)/5 = 0.616 g
  2. MFI = 0.616 × 600 / 30
  3. MFI = 12.3 g/10 min
MFI = 12.3 g/10 min, a high-flow injection-moulding grade. Longer chains (higher molecular weight) would flow less and give a lower MFI (Chapter 8).

Good laboratory practice and safety

Calibrate first

Use standards that bracket the samples; check with an independent control sample

Record conditions

Temperature, instrument, method, standard used, operator and date

Replicate

At least duplicates; report mean ± uncertainty with sensible significant figures

Sample properly

Oil from a running machine; water after flushing the sample line

Stay safe

Read the safety data sheet; eye protection and lab coat; add acid to water, never the reverse

Dispose correctly

Heavy metals and solvents are hazardous waste; never pour them down the sink

Takeaways

  • Every instrument needs calibration and an uncertainty
  • The pH meter is the Nernst equation; temperature matters
  • Choose indicators by the pH at equivalence
