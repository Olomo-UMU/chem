Polymers

Polymers are long chains of repeated small units, and the length and shape of the chains decide whether a plastic is soft, rigid, tough or clear. 8.1Monomers, polymers and molecular weight

A polymer is a very large molecule built from a small molecule, the monomer, repeated many times along a chain. A short chain of only a few units is an oligomer. Large molecules built from many different smaller units bound together, such as proteins and DNA, are called macromolecules. Natural polymers include cellulose, starch, natural rubber, cotton, silk, wool and keratin. Plastics are polymers shaped by melting and moulding, extruding or compressing.

The number of repeat units in a chain, the degree of polymerisation (DP), is typically between 103 and 106 for useful plastics. It cannot be controlled exactly, so every sample contains chains of many lengths and its molecular weight is an average. Two averages are used:

Copolymers

A copolymer is made from two or more different monomers, which can be arranged at random, alternately, in blocks, or as branches grafted onto a main chain. Copolymers tune properties: polyethylene turns brittle below about 0 °C, but with 15 mol % of vinyl acetate it stays flexible down to −40 ∘C. PVC is hard and brittle; it is softened either with a plasticiser (up to 30 % by weight of dioctyl phthalate for tubing) or by copolymerising it with about 5 % propylene. Plasticiser slowly escapes from vinyl upholstery, which is why old car seats crack.

8.2How polymers are made

Addition polymerisation

In addition (chain-growth) polymerisation, monomers with a C=C double bond add one after another to the end of a growing chain, and nothing is given off. The reaction is carried by free radicals, fragments with an unpaired electron (marked •). It has three stages:

R• comes from an initiator such as benzoyl peroxide, split by heat or by ultraviolet light (light-cured dental fillings work this way). M is the monomer.

Condensation polymerisation

In condensation (step-growth) polymerisation two different monomers, each with two reactive groups, join and give off a small molecule, usually water. The chain grows at both ends (A and B join to give AB, AB and A give ABA, ABA and B give BABA, and so on) until one monomer is used up. Nylon 6,6 is made from a six-carbon diamine and a six-carbon dicarboxylic acid; PET from a diacid and a diol.

8.3Engineering polymers

Many common plastics are vinyl polymers, made from monomers CHX2​=CHX: X = H gives polyethylene, CHX3​ polypropylene, Cl PVC and a benzene ring polystyrene. Polymers that soften on heating and can be remoulded are thermoplastics; those that set into a cross-linked network and char instead of melting are thermosets (Bakelite, epoxies); lightly cross-linked, rubbery polymers are elastomers.

PolymerDensity (g/cm³)Tg (°C)Tm (°C)Tensile strength (MPa)Typical engineering uses
  • Polyethylene (PE): HDPE has long, little-branched chains and high crystallinity, so it is strong: fuel tanks, pipes, hoses, thin-walled mouldings. LDPE has branched chains, lower strength and hardness, excellent water resistance: films, cable insulation.
  • Polypropylene (PP), produced since the 1950s with Ziegler–Natta catalysts: the lowest density of the major plastics (0.90 g/cm³), melts at 165 °C, rigid and chemically resistant: bumpers, battery cases, hoses. Expanded PP (EPP) absorbs impact energy: bumper cores, seats, headrests.
  • Polyvinyl chloride (PVC): chemically resistant, hard, self-extinguishing, a good insulator, easy to weld and glue: pipes, profiles, cable sheathing, artificial leather.
  • Polystyrene (PS) in general-purpose, high-impact (HIPS) and expanded (EPS) grades: light, rigid: instrument panels, trims, battery boxes, acoustic and thermal insulation foam.
  • Polyamides (PA, nylons): hard, tough, abrasion-resistant, low friction, crystallise fast: gears, bearings, bushes, cable ties, conveyor belts. PA 6,6 has Tg​ = 78 °C and Tm​ = 269 °C. They absorb some water, which changes their dimensions.
  • Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA, acrylic): transmits up to 92 % of light and weathers well: lamp covers, caravan and vehicle glazing.
  • Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS): a terpolymer, tough with a hard glossy surface that can be electroplated: dashboards, grilles, mirror housings, wheel covers.
  • Polyurethane (PUR): from soft foam to hard solid; absorbs vibration and sound: seats, padding, rollers, seals.
  • Polycarbonate (PC), mostly from bisphenol A: amorphous, Tg​ = 150 °C, very high impact strength, transparent, flame-retardant: headlamp lenses, safety glazing, visors, helmets.

8.4Plastic recycling

Plastics are cheap, light and durable, but that durability makes plastic waste a long-lived pollutant. Of all the plastic ever made up to 2015, only about 9 % had been recycled and 12 % incinerated; the rest was in landfill or the environment. The waste hierarchy is to reduce plastic use at source, substitute other materials where sensible, reuse, and then recycle, as part of a circular economy. Recycling falls into four classes:

  • Primary (closed loop): clean, sorted scrap is reprocessed into the same product.
  • Secondary (mechanical): mixed or used plastic is sorted, ground, washed, melted and remoulded into lower-grade products.
  • Tertiary (chemical): the chains are broken down into monomers or fuels that become feedstock again (Table 8.2).
  • Quaternary (energy recovery): incineration with heat recovery, for what cannot be sorted.
