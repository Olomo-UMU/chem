7.1Carbon bonding and hybridisation

Carbon bonds covalently to itself and to hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur and the halogens, so it can build structures from the simplest to the extremely complex. With the configuration 1s22s22p2 it shares four electrons to reach the configuration of neon, so a carbon atom in a stable molecule always has four bonds. These can be σ or π bonds, but they always add up to four.

The 2s and 2p orbitals of carbon are close in energy and can mix, a process called hybridisation:

  • sp3: the 2s orbital mixes with all three 2p orbitals, giving four hybrid orbitals at 109.5° (tetrahedral). Four single σ bonds, as in methane.
  • sp2: 2s mixes with two 2p orbitals, giving three hybrids at 120° (trigonal planar). The unused p orbital forms one π bond: a double bond, as in ethene.
  • sp: 2s mixes with one 2p orbital, giving two hybrids at 180° (linear). The two unused p orbitals form two π bonds: a triple bond, as in ethyne.

So the number of π bonds decides the hybridisation: none for sp3, one for sp2, two for sp. Hybrid orbitals plus π bonds always make four.

7.2Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons contain only carbon and hydrogen. Their carbon skeletons are straight chains (no carbon bonded to more than two others), branched chains or rings.

Alkanes

The alkanes, general formula Cn​H2n+2​, contain only sp3 carbons and single bonds. They are saturated: every carbon has the maximum four neighbours. With only C–C and C–H bonds they are nonpolar and chemically stable. Petroleum and natural gas are mainly mixtures of alkanes; the larger ones are waxy solids called paraffins.

Isomers

Structural isomers have the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms. From four carbons onward, alkanes have branched isomers. Butane, CX4​HX10​, can be a straight chain or isobutane, a three-carbon chain with a −CHX3​ group on the middle carbon. Pentane has three isomers, octane eighteen. Branching makes a molecule more compact, which lowers its boiling point and, in fuels, its tendency to knock.

Alkenes and alkynes

Alkenes, Cn​H2n​, contain a C=C double bond and are named with the ending -ene: ethene CX2​HX4​, propene CX3​HX6​, the butenes CX4​HX8​. They are unsaturated: each double bond is one site of unsaturation, with two fewer hydrogens than the alkane. From butene on, a number gives the position of the double bond (but-1-ene, but-2-ene). Because the double bond cannot rotate, an internal double bond gives cis and trans isomers, with the two methyl groups on the same side or on opposite sides. Molecules with two double bonds are dienes, with three trienes.

Alkynes, Cn​H2n−2​, contain a C≡C triple bond and end in -yne: ethyne (acetylene, HC≡CH, the fuel of oxy-acetylene welding), propyne, but-1-yne and but-2-yne. The sp carbons are linear, so alkynes have no cis and trans isomers.

Cyclic and aromatic hydrocarbons

Cycloalkanes, Cn​H2n​, are alkanes whose chain closes into a ring: cyclopropane, cyclobutane, cyclopentane, cyclohexane. They behave much like alkanes but are isomers of the alkenes. Aromatic hydrocarbons are rings of sp2 carbons in which the π electrons are delocalised around the ring. The simplest is benzene, CX6​HX6​: a flat hexagon usually drawn with alternating single and double bonds (a conjugated system), but in reality all six C–C bonds are the same, 139 pm, because the π electrons are shared by the whole ring. Delocalisation lowers the energy and makes aromatic rings unusually stable.

7.3Naming organic compounds

Organic compounds are named by the rules of the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC):

  1. Find the longest continuous carbon chain (the backbone); it gives the parent name from Table 7.1.
  2. Branches are alkyl groups, named by replacing -ane with -yl: methyl −CHX3​, ethyl −CHX2​CHX3​, propyl −CHX2​CHX2​CHX3​. A general alkyl group is written R.
  3. Number the chain from the end that gives the lowest numbers to the substituents (or to the main functional group).
  4. Write the position, a dash and the substituent before the parent name: 2-methylpropane. Several identical groups take di-, tri-, tetra-; different groups are listed alphabetically.
  5. When there are several functional groups, the one with the highest priority sets the ending: carboxylic acids and their derivatives, then aldehydes and ketones, then alcohols and amines, then double and triple bonds, then halides. The others become prefixes. So CHX2​ClCHX2​CHX2​CHX2​OH is 4-chlorobutan-1-ol.

7.4Functional groups

A functional group is a group of atoms or a type of bond that gives a molecule its characteristic reactions. The rest of the molecule, a fairly inert hydrocarbon skeleton, matters much less, so knowing the group lets you predict the chemistry.   • Alcohols are organic relatives of water, bent at the sp3 oxygen with two lone pairs. They hydrogen bond, so they boil much higher than the alkanes; polarity and water solubility fall as the chain grows. Methanol and ethanol are fuels; propan-2-ol (isopropyl alcohol) is a disinfectant and cleaner. The ending is -ol with the position of the –OH: propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol.
  • Amines are relatives of ammonia with one, two or three hydrogens replaced by alkyl groups (primary, secondary, tertiary). The nitrogen is trigonal pyramidal with one lone pair. Primary amines boil higher than alkanes but lower than alcohols. Names: ethanamine, propan-1-amine, N-methylpropan-1-amine, N,N-dimethylethanamine.
  • Organohalides R–X: the chlorinated methanes are chloromethane, dichloromethane, trichloromethane (chloroform) and tetrachloromethane (carbon tetrachloride). Several halogens are listed alphabetically: CFX2​ClX2​ is dichlorodifluoromethane, the old refrigerant R-12, banned because it destroys ozone.
  • Ethers R–O–R′ have no O–H, so they cannot hydrogen bond to each other and boil low. Named as alkoxy groups on the larger chain: methoxyethane, 2-methoxypropane.
  • Aldehydes and ketones share the carbonyl group C=O (sp2, trigonal planar). Methanal (formaldehyde) is used in resins; propanone (acetone) and butanone (methyl ethyl ketone) are solvents.
  • Carboxylic acids –COOH have both a carbonyl and a hydroxyl group: methanoic (formic) and ethanoic (acetic) acid. Their salts end in -oate: sodium ethanoate.
  • Esters –COOR form from an acid and an alcohol: ethyl methanoate, ethyl ethanoate.
  • Amides –CONH₂ replace the –OH of an acid with an amino group: ethanamide, N-methylpropanamide, N,N-dimethylpropanamide.

7.5Organic reactions

The reactions of the functional groups fall into a few types:

  • Substitution: one group replaces another, as when an alcohol reacts with HCl to give an alkyl chloride. Nucleophilic substitution runs by two routes: SN2, one step, for primary carbons; and SN1, through a positively charged carbocation, for tertiary carbons, where crowding by large groups (steric hindrance) blocks the one-step route.
  • Elimination: a small molecule is removed, leaving a double bond. Hot concentrated sulfuric acid dehydrates ethanol to ethene; strong bases eliminate HX from alkyl halides (E2 for primary, E1 through a carbocation for tertiary).
  • Addition: a molecule adds across a double or triple bond, which becomes single. Hydrogen adds to give alkanes (hydrogenation, used to harden vegetable oils); bromine adds to ethene, which decolourises bromine water and is the standard test for C=C.
  • Oxidation: alcohols oxidise to aldehydes, ketones and acids; complete oxidation (combustion) gives carbon dioxide and water (Chapter 9).
  • Condensation: two molecules join and release a small one, usually water, as in ester and amide formation. Repeated condensation builds polyesters and nylons (Chapter 8).
