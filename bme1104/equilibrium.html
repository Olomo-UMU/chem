4.1Reversible reactions

In a reversible reaction the reactants form products, and the products also react to give back the reactants. Almost every reaction is reversible to some extent, even when the back reaction is very slow. For the general reaction

aA+bB ⇌ cC+dD
The reaction to the right is the forward reaction, the one to the left is the reverse reaction. a, b, c and d are the coefficients of the balanced equation.

When the reactants are first mixed, the forward reaction is fast: products build up and reactants are used up. As the products accumulate, the reverse reaction speeds up while the forward reaction slows down. Eventually the two rates become equal and the concentrations stop changing. This is dynamic equilibrium: both reactions continue at the molecular level, but there is no overall change.

4.2The equilibrium constant

The equilibrium constant Keq​ relates the concentrations of products and reactants at equilibrium:

Keq​=[A]a[B]b[C]c[D]d​
Square brackets mean molar concentration (mol/L, written M). Each term is raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation.

The expression is written by these rules:

  1. All concentrations are equilibrium values.
  2. Products go in the numerator and reactants in the denominator.
  3. Each concentration is raised to the power of its stoichiometric coefficient.
  4. For a homogeneous reaction (all species in one phase, all gases or all in solution), every reactant and every product appears.
  5. For a heterogeneous reaction, a pure solid is left out, because its concentration is fixed by its density.
  6. A liquid that is both the solvent and a reactant or product (such as water in aqueous reactions) is also left out, because its concentration hardly changes.

A large Keq​ (well above 1) means that at equilibrium the mixture is mostly products; a small one means mostly reactants. For gases, partial pressures in atmospheres can be used instead. Since P=nRT/V=MRT,

Keq​(atm)=Keq​(M)(RT)Δn
Δn = (c + d) − (a + b), the change in the number of moles of gas. R = 0.08206 L atm/(K mol).

The constant for the reverse reaction is the reciprocal of the constant for the forward reaction:

Keq​(reverse)=Keq​(forward)1​

4.3Le Chatelier's principle and the reaction quotient

Le Chatelier's principle: when a reaction at equilibrium is subjected to a change in conditions, the position of the equilibrium shifts to counteract the change until a new equilibrium is established. The conditions that disturb an equilibrium are a change of concentration, of temperature, of volume (for gases) or of pressure (for gases).

Table 4.1: Response of an equilibrium to a change in conditions
Change in conditionResponseEffect on Keq​
Increase the concentration of a reactantMore products formNone
Increase the concentration of a productMore reactants formNone
Decrease the concentration of a reactantMore reactants formNone
Decrease the concentration of a productMore products formNone
Increase the volume (lower the pressure)Shift to the side with more moles of gasNone
Decrease the volume (raise the pressure)Shift to the side with fewer moles of gasNone
Increase the temperatureShift in the direction that absorbs heat (endothermic)Changes
Decrease the temperatureShift in the direction that releases heat (exothermic)Changes

Only a change of temperature changes the value of Keq​. To predict which way a disturbed mixture will move, use the reaction quotient Q, which has the same form as Keq​ but uses the concentrations at any moment:

Q=[A]a[B]b[C]c[D]d​
  • Q<Keq​: too few products, so more products form (shift to the right).
  • Q>Keq​: too many products, so more reactants form (shift to the left).
  • Q=Keq​: the mixture is already at equilibrium.

4.4Acids and bases

The Brønsted–Lowry definition

A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (HX+) donor and a base is a proton acceptor. An acid–base reaction transfers a proton from an acid to a base. The acid becomes its conjugate base and the base becomes its conjugate acid:

acidHA​+baseB​ ⇌ conjugate baseA−​+conjugate acidHB+​

A strong acid has a weak conjugate base and a weak acid a strong conjugate base (and the same for bases). Amphoteric species can act as either: water accepts a proton to give HX3​OX+ or gives one up to leave OHX−; others are HSOX4​X−, HX2​POX4​X−, HCOX3​X− and HPOX4​X2−. A monoprotic acid (HCl) donates one proton; polyprotic acids (diprotic HX2​SOX4​, triprotic HX3​POX4​) donate them in steps, each step less readily than the last.

The Lewis definition

A Lewis acid accepts a lone pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond (an electron-pair acceptor, or electrophile). A Lewis base donates a lone pair (an electron-pair donor, or nucleophile). The new bond is a coordinate (dative) bond and the product is an adduct, as in BFX3​+NHX3​​FX3​B−NHX3​. The definition covers many reactions without protons. Lewis acids include all cations, species with an incomplete octet such as BFX3​ and AlClX3​, and central atoms that can take more than eight electrons. Lewis bases include anions such as FX− and OHX−, molecules with lone pairs such as HX2​O and NHX3​, and electron-rich π systems such as ethene and benzene.

Acids and bases in water

The strength of an acid or base in water is the extent to which it ionises. A strong acid (HCl, HNOX3​, HX2​SOX4​) gives up its proton to water completely. Strong bases are the hydroxides of group 1 and the soluble hydroxides of group 2 (Ca, Sr, Ba), which dissociate completely. A weak acid ionises only partly, measured by the acid ionisation constant:

HA(aq)+HX2​O(l)​HX3​OX+(aq)+AX−(aq)Ka​=[HA][H3​O+][A−]​
Water is left out of the expression because, as the solvent, its concentration hardly changes. The larger the Ka, the stronger the acid.
B(aq)+HX2​O(l)​BHX+(aq)+OHX−(aq)Kb​=[B][BH+][OH−]​
Ammonia is the usual example of a weak base, Kb = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵.

Water itself ionises very slightly, and at 25 °C the product of the ion concentrations is always the same:

Kw​=[H3​O+][OH−]=1.0×10−14at 25 ∘C
So in neutral water [H₃O⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ M.

The pH scale

Because concentrations span many powers of ten, they are expressed as negative logarithms:

pH=−log[H3​O+]pOH=−log[OH−]pH+pOH=14pKa​=−logKa​
pH 7 is neutral at 25 °C; below 7 is acidic and above 7 is basic. One pH unit is a factor of ten in [H₃O⁺].

Titration

In a titration a solution of known concentration is run from a burette into a measured volume of the unknown until the reaction is just complete, the equivalence point, shown by an indicator changing colour. Phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and turns pink above about pH 8.2.

4.5Buffer solutions

A buffer resists changes of pH when small amounts of strong acid or base are added. It is usually a weak acid with its conjugate base in similar amounts, made by dissolving a salt of the conjugate base (sodium acetate) in a solution of the acid (acetic acid). The excess conjugate base mops up added acid, and the weak acid mops up added base:

AX−(aq)+HX3​OX+(aq)​HX2​O(l)+HA(aq)OHX−(aq)+HA(aq)​HX2​O(l)+AX−(aq)

Because the acid is barely ionised and the salt supplies nearly all of the AX−, the concentrations in the Ka​ expression can be taken as those made up. Taking logarithms gives the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation:

[H3​O+]=Ka​[A−][HA]​⟹pH=pKa​+log[HA][A−]​
The buffer works best when [A⁻] = [HA], where pH = pKa; its useful range is about pKa ± 1. Buffer capacity is the amount of acid or base it can absorb before it stops working.
